    Assessing model accuracy#

    How do we tell a good model from a bad one? This page introduces the standard score for regression, the mean squared error, and uses a cooling cup of tea to show the most important lesson in machine learning: a model must be judged on data it has not seen. That lesson leads to the bias–variance trade-off.

    The mean squared error#

    For a regression model the standard score is the mean squared error:

    \[ \mathrm{MSE} = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\bigr)^2 . \]

    Read it from the inside out. \(\hat f(x_i)\) is the prediction for observation \(i\); \(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\) is its error, measured minus predicted; squaring makes every error positive; and \(\frac1n\sum\) averages over the \(n\) observations. A small MSE means the predictions lie close to the data.

    Why square? Without squaring, positive and negative errors would cancel, and a terrible model could score zero. Squaring also punishes large errors most: an error of 3 counts nine times as much as an error of 1.

    Example. Errors of \(+1\), \(-2\) and \(+3\) \(^\circ\)C give

    \[ \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1^2 + (-2)^2 + 3^2}{3} = \frac{1 + 4 + 9}{3} \approx 4.67\ ^\circ\mathrm C^2 . \]

    The MSE is in squared units. Its square root, the RMSE, is back in the units of \(y\) and reads as a typical error.

    Example: the mean squared error by hand#

    A model predicts the temperature of a motor housing at five times. The measured and predicted values (°C) are

    measured \(y_i\)20.122.425.027.930.2
    predicted \(\hat y_i\)20.522.025.627.530.0

    Compute the mean squared error and its square root (the RMSE). What are the units of each?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Residuals \(y_i - \hat y_i\): -0.4, +0.4, -0.6, +0.4, +0.2. Their squares: 0.16, 0.16, 0.36, 0.16, 0.04.

    \[ \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{5}(0.16 + 0.16 + 0.36 + 0.16 + 0.04) = 0.176\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}^2, \qquad \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{0.176} = 0.420\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}. \]

    The MSE is in squared units; the RMSE is back in °C, so it reads as a typical error of about 0.420 °C.

    Example: fitting a cooling curve#

    A temperature sensor in a cup of tea records 15 readings as the tea cools. The data are simulated from Newton's law of cooling, \(T = 25 + 60\,e^{-t/8}\) \(^\circ\)C (room temperature 25 \(^\circ\)C, starting temperature 85 \(^\circ\)C), plus random noise with standard deviation \(\sigma = 2\) \(^\circ\)C.

    Now we pretend not to know the law, and fit polynomials in time instead:

    \[ \hat T = a_0 + a_1 t + a_2 t^2 + \dots + a_d t^d . \]

    The degree \(d\) is the highest power of \(t\). A polynomial of degree \(d\) has \(d + 1\) coefficients \(a_0, \dots, a_d\), so a higher degree means a more flexible curve.

    Which fit would you trust to predict the temperature at \(t = 26\) min, just beyond the data? The degree-12 curve has the smallest error on the 15 readings, yet near 26 minutes it swings above 70 \(^\circ\)C, which is physically absurd for a cooling cup. Degree 3 is the sensible choice. The next section makes this judgement precise.

    Training error versus test error#

    Two different MSEs can be computed for each fitted polynomial:

    • the training MSE, on the 15 readings used to fit the curve;
    • the test MSE, on 1000 new readings the curve never saw.
    degree \(d\)training MSE (\(^\circ\)C\(^2\))test MSE (\(^\circ\)C\(^2\))
    171.345.6
    32.55.0
    120.70168

    Four observations:

    1. The training MSE always falls as flexibility grows. A more flexible model can always get at least as close to the training points: from 71 at degree 1 to 0.7 at degree 12.
    2. The test MSE falls, then rises: a U-shape. At first, extra flexibility captures the real curve better and the test MSE falls (46 at degree 1, 5.0 at degree 3). Beyond that, the extra flexibility fits the noise in the 15 training points. That noise does not repeat in new data, so the test MSE rises (168 at degree 12).
    3. The test MSE never goes below \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = 2^2 = 4\). This is the irreducible error of the previous page: even the true cooling law would have test MSE 4. The best polynomial, degree 3, reaches 5.0, close to this floor.
    4. A small training MSE with a large test MSE is overfitting. At degree 12 the training MSE (0.7) is even below the noise variance. That is a warning sign: the model is memorising the noise.

    The rule that follows is the most important in machine learning: choose a model by its test error, not its training error. Lecture 4 shows how to estimate the test error when there is no separate test set (cross-validation).

    The bias–variance decomposition#

    Why is the test error U-shaped? Imagine repeating the whole experiment many times. Each repeat gives a new set of 15 noisy readings, hence a different fitted curve, hence a different prediction at some fixed new time \(x_0\). The expected value \(\mathbb E\) below averages over all these repeats. The average squared error at \(x_0\) splits into three parts, all positive:

    \[ \mathbb E\bigl(y_0 - \hat f(x_0)\bigr)^2 = \operatorname{Var}\bigl(\hat f(x_0)\bigr) + \bigl[\operatorname{Bias}\bigl(\hat f(x_0)\bigr)\bigr]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon). \]

    Here \(y_0\) is the measured output at \(x_0\), and:

    • Variance, \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))\): how much the prediction changes from one training set to another. A very flexible model follows every detail of its training data, so different training sets give very different curves: high variance.
    • Bias, \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat f(x_0)) = \mathbb E\hat f(x_0) - f(x_0)\): the average prediction minus the truth. It is the error from using a model that is too simple. A straight line cannot follow a curved cooling law however much data it is given; its average prediction is systematically off.
    • Noise, \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\): the irreducible error, as before.

    Underfitting and overfitting#

    The decomposition names the two ways a model can fail:

    • High bias, low variance: underfitting. The model is too simple to follow \(f\), like the straight line on the cooling data. It is stable, but wrong.
    • Low bias, high variance: overfitting. The model follows the noise, like the degree-12 polynomial. It is right on average, but unstable: each training set gives a wildly different curve.

    More flexibility lowers bias but raises variance. The best model balances the two, at the bottom of the U. The noise term cannot be reduced by any choice of model.
