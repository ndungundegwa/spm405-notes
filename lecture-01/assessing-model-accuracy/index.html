    Assessing model accuracy#

    Measuring the quality of fit: the mean squared error#

    Mean squared error on the training data: \(\mathrm{MSE} = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \hat f(x_i))^2\)

    The standard score for a regression model.

    In words: for each observation, measured minus predicted, squared; then the average

    Read the formula from the inside out: \(\hat f(x_i)\) is the prediction, \(y_i - \hat f(x_i)\) the error, the square makes it positive, and \(\frac1n\sum\) averages over the observations.

    Small MSE: predictions close to the data. Squaring makes every error positive and punishes large errors most

    Without squaring, positive and negative errors would cancel. An error of 3 counts 9 times as much as an error of 1.

    Example: errors \(+1\), \(-2\), \(+3\) \(^\circ\)C: \(\mathrm{MSE} = (1 + 4 + 9)/3 \approx 4.7\) \(^\circ\)C\(^2\)

    Worked numbers

    \(1^2 = 1\), \((-2)^2 = 4\), \(3^2 = 9\); sum 14; divided by \(n = 3\) gives 4.67 \(^\circ\)C\(^2\).

    Symbols

    \(y_i\)Measured output of observation \(i\).
    \(\hat f(x_i)\)The model's prediction for observation \(i\).
    \(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\)Average over the \(n\) observations.
    MSEMean squared error. Units of \(y\) squared (\(^\circ\)C\(^2\)).

    Example: the mean squared error by hand#

    A model predicts the temperature of a motor housing at five times. The measured and predicted values (°C) are

    measured \(y_i\)20.122.425.027.930.2
    predicted \(\hat y_i\)20.522.025.627.530.0

    Compute the mean squared error and its square root (the RMSE). What are the units of each?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Residuals \(y_i - \hat y_i\): -0.4, +0.4, -0.6, +0.4, +0.2. Their squares: 0.16, 0.16, 0.36, 0.16, 0.04.

    \[ \mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{5}(0.16 + 0.16 + 0.36 + 0.16 + 0.04) = 0.176\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}^2, \qquad \mathrm{RMSE} = \sqrt{0.176} = 0.420\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}. \]

    The MSE is in squared units; the RMSE is back in °C, so it reads as a typical error of about 0.420 °C.

    Example: fitting a cooling curve#

    15 readings from a temperature sensor in a cooling cup of tea (simulated, noise \(\sigma = 2\) \(^\circ\)C), fitted with polynomials \(\hat T = a_0 + a_1t + a_2t^2 + \dots + a_dt^d\). The degree \(d\) is the highest power: a higher degree is a more flexible curve

    The data follow Newton's law of cooling (slide 29), \(T = 25 + 60\,e^{-t/8}\) \(^\circ\)C, plus random noise with standard deviation 2 \(^\circ\)C. We pretend not to know the law and fit polynomials instead. A polynomial of degree \(d\) has \(d + 1\) parameters \(a_0, \dots, a_d\); more parameters give a more flexible curve.

    Plots: degree 1 (straight line), degree 3, degree 12

    Degree 1 (a straight line) is too stiff: it misses the curve. Degree 3 follows the cooling curve well. Degree 12 passes very close to every point, but wiggles wildly between them, especially near the end.

    Which fit would you trust to predict the temperature at \(t = 26\) min?

    Degree 12 has the smallest training MSE, but near 26 minutes it swings to over 70 \(^\circ\)C, which is physically absurd for a cooling cup. Degree 3 is the sensible choice. The next slide makes this precise.

    Symbols

    \(\hat T\)Predicted temperature.
    \(a_0, \dots, a_d\)The parameters of the polynomial.
    \(d\)Degree: the highest power of \(t\).
    \(\sigma\)Standard deviation of the noise (here 2 \(^\circ\)C).

    Training MSE versus test MSE#

    Training MSE: on the 15 readings used to fit. Test MSE: on 1000 new readings

    We fit each polynomial to the 15 training readings, and then measure its MSE both on those same readings (training MSE) and on 1000 new readings it never saw (test MSE).

    Plot: training MSE and test MSE against flexibility (polynomial degree 1–12), log scale, with \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = 4\)

    The vertical axis is logarithmic: each gridline is ten times the one below, so values from 0.7 to 300 fit on one plot.

    Training MSE always falls as flexibility grows

    A more flexible model can always get at least as close to the training points: from 71 at degree 1 down to 0.7 at degree 12.

    Test MSE falls, then rises: a U-shape

    At first more flexibility captures the real curve better and the test MSE falls (46 at degree 1, 5.0 at degree 3). Beyond that, the extra flexibility fits the noise in the 15 training points, which does not repeat in new data, so the test MSE rises (168 at degree 12).

    Test MSE never goes below \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = 2^2 = 4\)

    The irreducible error of slide 26. Even the true curve would have test MSE 4 (green line). The best model, degree 3, gets to 5.0, close to this floor.

    Small training MSE with large test MSE is overfitting

    Degree 12: training MSE 0.7 (below the noise level!) but test MSE 168. A training MSE below the noise variance is a warning sign that the model is memorising noise.

    Key idea: choose the model by its test error

    The most important rule of machine learning. Lecture 4 shows how to estimate the test error when there is no separate test set (cross-validation).

    Symbols

    Training MSEMSE on the data used to fit the model.
    Test MSEMSE on new data not used in fitting.
    \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\)Noise variance: here \(2^2 = 4\) \(^\circ\)C\(^2\).

    Worked numbers

    From the plot: degree 1: training 71.3, test 45.6; degree 3: training 2.5, test 5.0; degree 12: training 0.70, test 168.

    The bias–variance decomposition#

    Imagine repeating the experiment many times, each with a new training set, and predicting at the same new input \(x_0\)

    Each repeat gives a different set of 15 noisy readings, so a different fitted curve, so a different prediction at \(x_0\). The expected value \(\mathbb E\) averages over all these repeats (slide 24).

    \(\mathbb E(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2 = \operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0)) + [\operatorname{Bias}(\hat f(x_0))]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\)

    The average squared error at \(x_0\) splits into three parts, all positive. This explains the U-shape of the test MSE.

    Variance: how much the prediction changes from one training set to another

    A very flexible model follows every detail of its training data, so different training sets give very different curves: high variance.

    Bias: average prediction minus the true \(f(x_0)\); the error from using a model that is too simple

    A straight line cannot follow a curved cooling law, however much data it has: its average prediction is systematically off. That systematic error is the bias.

    \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\): the noise: the irreducible error, as before

    As on slide 26.

    Symbols

    \(x_0\), \(y_0\)A new input and its (noisy) output.
    \(\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))\)Variance of the prediction over different training sets.
    \(\operatorname{Bias}(\hat f(x_0))\)Average prediction minus the true \(f(x_0)\).

    Underfitting and overfitting#

    Sketch: bias\(^2\) falls and variance rises with flexibility; their sum plus \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\) is the U-shaped test MSE

    The curves are drawn by hand to show the shapes, not computed from data. The noise line is flat: flexibility does not change it.

    High bias, low variance \(\Rightarrow\) underfitting: too simple to follow \(f\) (the straight line on the cooling data)

    Stable but wrong.

    Low bias, high variance \(\Rightarrow\) overfitting: follows the noise (degree 12)

    Right on average, but unstable: each training set gives a wildly different curve.

    More flexibility lowers bias but raises variance. The best model balances the two: the bottom of the U

    As flexibility grows, bias falls and variance rises; their sum is smallest in between. That is the bottom of the U. The noise term cannot be reduced.
