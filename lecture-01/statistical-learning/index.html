    Statistical learning#

    Two words from statistics: average and variance#

    The next slides use the symbols \(\mathbb E\) and \(\operatorname{Var}\). Many students last met them in a first-year statistics course, so they are defined here, with an example, before they are used.

    Expected value (long-run average): \(\mathbb E(Z) \approx (z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_m)/m\) for a large number of repeats \(m\)

    A random quantity \(Z\) takes a different value each time it is measured. Its expected value is the average we would get if we measured it very many times. “Approximately” because the average of a finite number of repeats is only close to it.

    Variance (spread about the average): \(\operatorname{Var}(Z) = \mathbb E(Z - \mathbb EZ)^2\)

    Take each value's distance from the average, square it, and average. A large variance means the values scatter widely.

    Standard deviation: \(\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z)}\), in the same units as \(Z\)

    The variance is in squared units (\(^\circ\)C\(^2\)); its square root, the standard deviation, is back in the units of \(Z\) and gives the typical size of the scatter.

    Example: readings of a 50 \(^\circ\)C cup with noise \(\sigma = 2\) \(^\circ\)C: average 50 \(^\circ\)C, variance 4 \(^\circ\)C\(^2\)

    A thermometer with random noise of standard deviation 2 \(^\circ\)C reads 49.1, 51.8, 50.6, …; the readings average to 50 \(^\circ\)C and their variance is \(2^2 = 4\) \(^\circ\)C\(^2\). The same numbers appear in the cooling-curve example (slide 33).

    Symbols

    \(Z\); \(z_1, z_2, \dots\)A random quantity; its measured values.
    \(\mathbb E\)Expected value: the average over very many repeats.
    \(\operatorname{Var}\)Variance: the average squared distance from the average.
    \(\sigma\)Sigma: standard deviation, \(\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}}\).

    Example: average and variance of mains voltage#

    Five readings of the mains voltage are 229.0, 231.5, 228.2, 232.1 and 230.2 V. Find the average, the variance (divide by \(n\)) and the standard deviation.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Average: \(\bar v = (229.0 + 231.5 + 228.2 + 232.1 + 230.2)/5 = 230.20\) V.

    Deviations from the average: -1.20, +1.30, -2.00, +1.90, +0.00. Squared: 1.4400, 1.6900, 4.0000, 3.6100, 0.0000.

    Variance \(= (1.4400 + 1.6900 + 4.0000 + 3.6100 + 0.0000)/5 = 2.1480\) V², standard deviation \(= \sqrt{2.1480} = 1.466\) V.

    Statistical learning: estimating \(f\)#

    Inputs \(X = (X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p)\), output \(Y\). We assume \(Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\)

    The central equation of the course. The output is some function of the inputs plus random error. Machine learning is the art of estimating \(f\) from data.

    Learning means finding an estimate \(\hat f\) of \(f\) from data, then predicting \(\hat Y = \hat f(X)\)

    The hat means “estimated from data”. \(\hat f\) is the model.

    Capital \(X\), \(Y\): the quantities in general. Small \(x_i\), \(y_i\): one measured observation

    \(Y\) is “panel power” in general; \(y_3 = 78.5\) W is the power in reading 3.

    \(f\) is the true relation in nature. It exists, but we do not know it. \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon) includes measurement noise and everything we did not measure. “Unrelated to \(X\)” (independent): the noise does not depend on the inputs. “Average zero”: it is as likely to be positive as negative, so \(\mathbb E(\varepsilon) = 0\).

    Solar panel: \(X_1\) = irradiance, \(X_2\) = cell temperature, \(Y\) = power. \(\varepsilon\): measurement noise and effects we did not measure (dust, wind). No model can predict it from \(X\)

    For the solar panel, dust on the glass or wind cooling it change the power, but we did not measure them, so their effect is part of the noise.

    Symbols

    \(X\), \(X_j\)Inputs; \(X_j\) is the \(j\)-th input.
    \(Y\)Output.
    \(f\)True, unknown relation between \(X\) and \(Y\).
    \(\varepsilon\)Epsilon: random error, mean zero.
    \(\hat f\), \(\hat Y\)Estimated function and its prediction.

    Reducible and irreducible error#

    The average squared prediction error splits into two parts: \(\mathbb E(Y - \hat Y)^2 = [f(X) - \hat f(X)]^2 + \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\): reducible + irreducible

    The left side is the average, over many new observations, of (measured \(-\) predicted)\(^2\).

    Reducible: our \(\hat f\) is not the true \(f\). A better method or more data can shrink it

    \([f - \hat f]^2\) comes from our estimate being imperfect.

    Irreducible: the noise \(\varepsilon\) itself. No method can remove it

    \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\) is the noise; it is not predictable from \(X\).

    Key idea: even the perfect model, \(\hat f = f\), has average squared error \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\)

    Put \(\hat f = f\) in the equation: the first term is zero and only the noise remains. This sets a floor that no model can go below; we see it in the data on slide 34.

    Symbols

    \(\mathbb E(Y - \hat Y)^2\)Average squared prediction error.
    \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\)Variance of the noise, \(\sigma^2\).
    Beyond the slide: where the equation comes from

    With \(Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\) and \(\hat Y = \hat f(X)\) for a fixed \(X\): \(\mathbb E(Y - \hat Y)^2 = \mathbb E(f - \hat f + \varepsilon)^2 = (f - \hat f)^2 + 2(f - \hat f)\mathbb E(\varepsilon) + \mathbb E(\varepsilon^2)\). The middle term is zero because \(\mathbb E(\varepsilon) = 0\), and \(\mathbb E(\varepsilon^2) = \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\).

    Why estimate \(f\)? Prediction and inference#

    Prediction: we want accurate predictions \(\hat Y\). How \(\hat f\) works inside does not matter: it can be a black box. Example: forecast tomorrow's panel output for the grid operator

    If we only need good predictions, it does not matter how \(\hat f\) works inside. A “black box” is a model whose inner workings we do not look at.

    Inference: we want to understand how \(Y\) depends on each input \(X_j\). \(\hat f\) must be simple enough to read. Example: how much power is lost per degree of heating? (Lecture 2)

    If we want understanding, \(\hat f\) must be interpretable. Solar cells lose efficiency as they heat up; a simple model of the panel gives this loss as one parameter, answered in Lecture 2.

    Parametric and non-parametric methods#

    Parametric: 1. Assume a form for \(f\), e.g. linear: \(f(X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \dots + \beta_pX_p\). 2. Fit it: estimate \(\beta_0, \dots, \beta_p\) from data

    A parametric method first chooses a formula with a fixed number of unknown parameters, then estimates them. Learning \(f\) becomes learning a few numbers.

    \(+\) only \(p + 1\) numbers to estimate; \(-\) if the assumed form is wrong, the fit is poor

    Few parameters need little data and are easy to interpret. But if nature is not linear, no choice of the \(\beta\)'s fixes that.

    Non-parametric: assume no fixed form; follow the data as closely as is reasonable. Examples: \(K\)-nearest neighbours (later today), decision trees (Lecture 5)

    These methods let the data decide the shape of \(f\).

    \(+\) can fit many shapes of \(f\); \(-\) needs many more observations; \(-\) harder to interpret; can follow the noise

    Flexibility is powerful but costly: more data are needed, and the model may fit the noise (overfitting, slide 34).

    Symbols

    \(\beta_0, \dots, \beta_p\)Beta nought, beta one, …: the parameters. \(\beta_0\) is \(f\) when all inputs are zero; \(\beta_j\) is the change in \(f\) per unit of \(X_j\).

    Physics gives us parametric forms#

    Newton's law of cooling: \(T(t) = T_{\mathrm{env}} + (T_0 - T_{\mathrm{env}})\,e^{-kt}\). Three parameters: \(T_{\mathrm{env}}\), \(T_0\), \(k\)

    Physics often supplies the right formula. Newton's law of cooling has three parameters: the surrounding temperature \(T_{\mathrm{env}}\), the starting temperature \(T_0\), and the cooling rate \(k\). At \(t = 0\), \(e^0 = 1\) and \(T = T_0\); as \(t\) grows, \(e^{-kt} \to 0\) and \(T \to T_{\mathrm{env}}\).

    Key idea: know the law? Fit its parameters. No law? A flexible method may do better

    With the right law, a parametric fit is best: three numbers, each with a physical meaning. Without one, a flexible method may win. The cooling cup returns on slide 33, fitted without the law.

    Symbols

    \(T(t)\)Temperature at time \(t\) (\(^\circ\)C).
    \(t\)Time since the start (min).
    \(T_{\mathrm{env}}\)Temperature of the surroundings.
    \(T_0\)Temperature at \(t = 0\).
    \(k\)Cooling rate constant (per min).
    \(e^{-kt}\)Exponential decay from 1 towards 0.

    Flexibility versus interpretability#

    Chart: methods placed by flexibility (horizontal) and interpretability (vertical): subset selection, lasso, least squares (top left); generalised additive models, trees (middle); bagging, boosting, support vector machines, deep learning (bottom right)

    The chart, from ISLP, shows a trade-off. Methods at the top left are simple and easy to interpret but can only fit simple shapes. Methods at the bottom right can fit almost anything but are hard to interpret.

    Flexibility: how many shapes of \(f\) a method can fit. Interpretability: how easily we can read what the model has learned

    A straight line is inflexible but easy to read: its slope is one number with a meaning. A deep network can fit almost any shape, but its millions of parameters have no individual meaning.

    The names are only labels for now; each method comes in Lectures 2–7

    Least squares and subset selection: Lectures 2 and 4; lasso: Lecture 4; additive models and trees: Lecture 5; support vector machines: Lecture 6; deep learning: Lecture 7. Students do not need to know them yet.

    Inference: choose a less flexible model. Each parameter has a meaning you can check against physics

    If the aim is understanding, use a simple model whose parameters have physical meaning.

    Prediction: a more flexible model may help, but not always: very flexible models can overfit, following the noise instead of \(f\)

    Even for prediction, more flexibility is not automatically better. This is the subject of slides 33–36.

    Supervised vs. unsupervised; regression vs. classification#

    Supervised: each \(x_i\) has a label \(y_i\). Goal: predict \(y\) for new \(x\), or understand how \(y\) depends on \(x\). Linear and logistic regression, SVMs, boosting, neural networks

    As on slide 20, now in symbols.

    Unsupervised: no \(y_i\). Goal: find groups or structure in the \(x_i\). Clustering, principal component analysis (PCA): Lecture 8

    Clustering finds groups; PCA finds the few combinations of inputs that carry most of the variation.

    Regression: \(y\) is quantitative (a number). Electricity use (kWh) of an area; the true temperature from a drifting sensor's reading

    The output is a number on a scale. Correcting a drifting sensor is a regression: from its raw reading (and perhaps its age and the ambient temperature) predict the true value measured by a reference instrument.

    Classification: \(y\) is qualitative (a category, also called a class). Healthy or faulty machine; adulterated or pure milk

    The output is a label from a fixed list. Milk adulteration was named in the survey: from measured properties (density, conductivity, spectrum) decide whether a sample is pure.

    The boundary can blur: logistic regression predicts a class probability, which is a number

    Logistic regression (Lecture 3) is a classification method, yet its output is a probability, which is a number.
