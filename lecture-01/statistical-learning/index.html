    Statistical learning#

    This page sets up the mathematical picture behind every method in the course: an output that depends on some inputs through an unknown function, plus random noise. Learning means estimating that function from data. We first recall two words from statistics, the expected value and the variance, because the rest of the course uses them constantly.

    Expected value and variance#

    A random quantity \(Z\) takes a different value each time it is measured, like the reading of a noisy thermometer. We write its measured values as \(z_1, z_2, \dots\).

    The expected value \(\mathbb E(Z)\) is the average we would get if we measured \(Z\) very many times:

    \[ \mathbb E(Z) \approx \frac{z_1 + z_2 + \dots + z_m}{m} \qquad \text{for a large number of repeats } m . \]

    (The sign \(\approx\) is there because a finite number of repeats only gets close to the long-run average.)

    The variance measures the spread about that average. Take each value's distance from the average, square it, and average the squares:

    \[ \operatorname{Var}(Z) = \mathbb E\bigl(Z - \mathbb E(Z)\bigr)^2 . \]

    A large variance means the values scatter widely. The variance is in squared units (for temperatures, \(^\circ\)C\(^2\)), so we often use its square root, the standard deviation

    \[ \sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(Z)}, \]

    which is back in the units of \(Z\) and gives the typical size of the scatter.

    Example. A thermometer with random noise of standard deviation \(\sigma = 2\) \(^\circ\)C measures a cup at 50 \(^\circ\)C. It reads 49.1, 51.8, 50.6, … The readings average to \(\mathbb E(Z) = 50\) \(^\circ\)C and their variance is \(\sigma^2 = 2^2 = 4\) \(^\circ\)C\(^2\).

    Example: average and variance of mains voltage#

    Five readings of the mains voltage are 229.0, 231.5, 228.2, 232.1 and 230.2 V. Find the average, the variance (divide by \(n\)) and the standard deviation.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Average: \(\bar v = (229.0 + 231.5 + 228.2 + 232.1 + 230.2)/5 = 230.20\) V.

    Deviations from the average: -1.20, +1.30, -2.00, +1.90, +0.00. Squared: 1.4400, 1.6900, 4.0000, 3.6100, 0.0000.

    Variance \(= (1.4400 + 1.6900 + 4.0000 + 3.6100 + 0.0000)/5 = 2.1480\) V², standard deviation \(= \sqrt{2.1480} = 1.466\) V.

    The central equation: \(Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\)#

    Suppose we measure \(p\) inputs \(X = (X_1, X_2, \dots, X_p)\) and one output \(Y\). We assume

    \[ Y = f(X) + \varepsilon . \]
    • \(f\) is the true relation between the inputs and the output. It exists in nature, but we do not know it.
    • \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon) is the random error: measurement noise and the effect of everything we did not measure. We assume it has average zero, \(\mathbb E(\varepsilon) = 0\) (it is as likely to push \(Y\) up as down), and that it is unrelated to \(X\), so it cannot be predicted from the inputs.

    Learning means finding an estimate \(\hat f\) of \(f\) from data, and then predicting \(\hat Y = \hat f(X)\) for new inputs. The hat means “estimated from data”; \(\hat f\) is our model.

    Capital letters \(X\), \(Y\) denote the quantities in general; small letters \(x_i\), \(y_i\) denote one measured observation. So \(Y\) is “panel power” in general, while \(y_3 = 78.5\) W is the power in reading 3.

    Example: the solar panel. \(X_1\) is the irradiance, \(X_2\) the cell temperature and \(Y\) the power. Dust on the glass and wind cooling the panel also change the power, but we did not measure them, so their effect is part of \(\varepsilon\). No model built from \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) can predict it.

    Reducible and irreducible error#

    How well can we predict? Measure it by the average squared prediction error over many new observations, \(\mathbb E(Y - \hat Y)^2\): the average of (measured \(-\) predicted)\(^2\). For a fixed input \(X\) it splits into two parts:

    \[ \mathbb E\bigl(Y - \hat Y\bigr)^2 = \underbrace{\bigl[f(X) - \hat f(X)\bigr]^2}_{\text{reducible}} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)}_{\text{irreducible}} . \]
    • The reducible error \([f - \hat f]^2\) arises because our estimate \(\hat f\) is not the true \(f\). A better method or more data can make it smaller.
    • The irreducible error \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2\) is the variance of the noise itself. No method can remove it, because the noise cannot be predicted from \(X\).

    The key consequence: even a perfect model, \(\hat f = f\), has average squared error \(\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\). Put \(\hat f = f\) in the equation and only the noise term remains. The noise sets a floor that no model can go below; we will see this floor in real numbers on the next page.

    Where the equation comes from

    With \(Y = f(X) + \varepsilon\) and \(\hat Y = \hat f(X)\) for a fixed \(X\), expand the square:

    \[ \mathbb E(Y - \hat Y)^2 = \mathbb E(f - \hat f + \varepsilon)^2 = (f - \hat f)^2 + 2(f - \hat f)\,\mathbb E(\varepsilon) + \mathbb E(\varepsilon^2). \]

    The middle term is zero because \(\mathbb E(\varepsilon) = 0\), and \(\mathbb E(\varepsilon^2) = \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\) because \(\varepsilon\) has average zero.

    Why estimate \(f\)? Prediction and inference#

    There are two reasons for estimating \(f\), and they lead to different choices of model.

    • Prediction. We want accurate predictions \(\hat Y\), and how \(\hat f\) works inside does not matter. It may be a black box, a model whose inner workings we never look at. Example: forecast tomorrow's panel output for the grid operator.
    • Inference. We want to understand how \(Y\) depends on each input \(X_j\). Then \(\hat f\) must be simple enough to read. Example: solar cells lose efficiency as they heat up; how many watts are lost per degree? A simple model gives the answer as a single parameter (Lecture 2).

    Parametric and non-parametric methods#

    There are two broad ways to estimate \(f\).

    Parametric methods work in two steps.

    1. Assume a form for \(f\) with a fixed number of unknown parameters. The most common is the linear form \[ f(X) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_p X_p . \] Here \(\beta_0\) (“beta nought”) is the value of \(f\) when all inputs are zero, and \(\beta_j\) is how much \(f\) changes when \(X_j\) increases by one unit with the other inputs held fixed.
    2. Fit the model: estimate the parameters \(\beta_0, \dots, \beta_p\) from the data.

    Learning \(f\) thus becomes learning \(p + 1\) numbers. That needs little data, and the numbers are easy to interpret. The weakness: if the assumed form is wrong (nature is not linear), no choice of the \(\beta\)'s can repair it, and the fit is poor.

    Non-parametric methods assume no fixed formula for \(f\); they follow the data as closely as is reasonable. Examples are \(K\)-nearest neighbours (Classification) and decision trees (Lecture 5). They can fit many shapes of \(f\), but they need many more observations, they are harder to interpret, and they can end up following the noise (overfitting, explained on the next page).

    Physics often supplies the form#

    In physics we frequently know the formula and only need its constants. Newton's law of cooling says that a hot object cools towards the temperature of its surroundings as

    \[ T(t) = T_{\mathrm{env}} + \bigl(T_0 - T_{\mathrm{env}}\bigr)\, e^{-kt}, \]

    where

    • \(T(t)\) is the temperature (\(^\circ\)C) at time \(t\) (minutes since the start),
    • \(T_{\mathrm{env}}\) is the temperature of the surroundings,
    • \(T_0\) is the temperature at \(t = 0\),
    • \(k\) is the cooling rate (per minute): a larger \(k\) means faster cooling,
    • \(e^{-kt}\) is an exponential decay that falls from 1 at \(t = 0\) towards 0.

    Check the two ends: at \(t = 0\), \(e^{0} = 1\) and \(T = T_0\); as \(t\) grows, \(e^{-kt} \to 0\) and \(T \to T_{\mathrm{env}}\). The model has three parameters, \(T_{\mathrm{env}}\), \(T_0\) and \(k\), each with a physical meaning.

    The lesson: if you know the law, fit its parameters. With the right formula a parametric fit is the best you can do. Without a law, a flexible method may do better. The same cooling cup returns on the next page, fitted without using the law.

    Flexibility versus interpretability#

    Two properties of a method pull in opposite directions:

    • Flexibility: how many different shapes of \(f\) the method can fit.
    • Interpretability: how easily we can read what the model has learned.

    A straight line is inflexible but easy to read: its slope is a single number with a meaning. A deep neural network can fit almost any shape, but its millions of parameters have no individual meaning. (The method names in the chart are introduced in Lectures 2–7.)

    How to choose:

    • For inference, choose a less flexible model, so that each parameter has a meaning you can check against physics.
    • For prediction, a more flexible model may help, but not always: very flexible models can overfit, following the noise instead of \(f\). The next page shows this happening.

    Supervised and unsupervised; regression and classification#

    In the notation of this page:

    • Supervised learning: every input \(x_i\) comes with a label \(y_i\). The goal is to predict \(y\) for new \(x\), or to understand how \(y\) depends on \(x\). Methods: linear and logistic regression, support vector machines, boosting, neural networks.
    • Unsupervised learning: there are no \(y_i\). The goal is to find groups or structure among the \(x_i\). Methods: clustering, which finds groups, and principal components analysis (PCA), which finds the few combinations of inputs that carry most of the variation (Lecture 8).

    Supervised problems are further divided by the type of output:

    • Regression: \(y\) is quantitative, a number on a scale. Examples: the electricity use (kWh) of an area; the true temperature, predicted from the raw reading of a drifting sensor (and perhaps its age and the ambient temperature).
    • Classification: \(y\) is qualitative, a category from a fixed list (also called a class). Examples: a machine is healthy or faulty; a milk sample is pure or adulterated, decided from its density, conductivity or spectrum.

    The boundary can blur. Logistic regression (Lecture 3) is a classification method, yet its output is the probability of a class, which is a number.
