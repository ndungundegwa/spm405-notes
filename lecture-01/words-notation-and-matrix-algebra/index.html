    The notation of ISLP, used in every lecture of the course. The slide's Symbols box gives each symbol in words.

    Matrix \(\bX\) with entries \(x_{11}, x_{12}, \dots, x_{np}\)

    All inputs in one table: \(n\) rows (observations) and \(p\) columns (inputs). Bold capital letters are matrices.

    Two subscripts: the first (\(i\)) says which observation (row), the second (\(j\)) which input (column). Row \(i\) is everything we measured about observation \(i\): a list of \(p\) numbers. Together the \(y_i\) form a column of length \(n\).

    Example: a solar panel: \(i\), irradiance \(x_{i1}\) (W/m\(^2\)), cell temperature \(x_{i2}\) (\(^\circ\)C), power \(y_i\) (W); rows \((260, 31.1, 70.3)\), \((270, 31.2, 86.4)\), \((270, 21.9, 78.5)\), …, \((950, 60.4, 231.4)\). Here \(n = 20\), \(p = 2\); \(x_{21} = 270\), \(y_3 = 78.5\). Irradiance: sunlight power falling on each square metre

    Each row is one reading of the panel: the sunlight falling on it, its temperature, and its electrical output. 20 readings of two inputs. \(x_{21}\) is row 2, column 1: the irradiance of reading 2. \(y_3\) is the power of reading 3. This panel returns in Lecture 2.

    Try it: Ask for \(x_{32}\) (21.9 \(^\circ\)C) and \(y_1\) (70.3 W).

    Symbols

    \(n\)Number of observations (rows).
    \(p\)Number of inputs (columns).
    \(x_{ij}\)Input \(j\) of observation \(i\).
    \(\bX\)Data matrix, \(n\times p\).
    \(x_i\)Row \(i\): all inputs of observation \(i\).
    \(y_i\)Output (label) of observation \(i\).
    Beyond the slide

    ISLP also writes \(x_i \in \mathbb R^p\) (“\(x_i\) is a list of \(p\) real numbers”) and uses bold \(\mathbf x_j\) for column \(j\), one input measured over all observations. These appear in later lectures.

    Matrix algebra: transpose and product#

    Transpose: swap rows and columns, \((\bX\T)_{ij} = x_{ji}\)

    The transpose turns rows into columns. An \(n\times p\) matrix becomes \(p\times n\).

    Product of \(A\) (\(r\times d\)) and \(B\) (\(d\times s\)): \((AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^d a_{ik}b_{kj}\), size \(r\times s\)

    To get entry \((i, j)\) of the product, go along row \(i\) of \(A\) and down column \(j\) of \(B\), multiply pairs and add. The sum sign \(\sum_{k=1}^d\) is shorthand for adding the terms for \(k = 1, 2, \dots, d\).

    Example: \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}5&6\\7&8\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}19&22\\43&50\end{pmatrix}\), e.g. \((AB)_{11} = 1\cdot5 + 2\cdot7 = 19\)

    A worked example to do together.

    Rule for sizes: \((r\times d)(d\times s) = r\times s\). The inner sizes must match

    The inner sizes (\(d\) and \(d\)) must be equal, or the product is not defined; the result has the outer sizes. Checking sizes catches most coding errors.

    Symbols

    \(\bX\T\)Transpose of \(\bX\).
    \(r\times d\)A matrix with \(r\) rows and \(d\) columns.
    \(a_{ik}\)Entry in row \(i\), column \(k\) of \(A\).
    \(\sum_{k=1}^d\)Sum over \(k\) from 1 to \(d\).

    Worked numbers

    \((AB)_{12} = 1\cdot6 + 2\cdot8 = 22\); \((AB)_{21} = 3\cdot5 + 4\cdot7 = 43\); \((AB)_{22} = 3\cdot6 + 4\cdot8 = 50\).

    Why matrices? All predictions at once#

    A linear model predicts every observation with one product: \(\hat{\mathbf y} = \bX\boldsymbol\beta\) (\(n\) predictions \(=\) \(n\times p\) inputs times \(p\) parameters). Check the sizes: \((n\times p)(p\times 1) = n\times 1\)

    A linear model multiplies each input by a parameter and adds the results. Writing the parameters as a column \(\boldsymbol\beta\), one matrix product gives the predictions for all \(n\) observations at once.

    Row \(i\) of the product: \(\hat y_i = x_{i1}\beta_1 + x_{i2}\beta_2 + \dots + x_{ip}\beta_p\)

    This is the product rule of slide 22 applied to row \(i\) of \(\bX\) and the single column \(\boldsymbol\beta\).

    In Python (NumPy): X.T is the transpose, X @ beta the product

    NumPy's notation; students meet it in the notebook.

    Solar panel: \(n = 20\), \(p = 2\): a \(20\times 2\) table times 2 parameters gives 20 predicted powers

    The sizes check: \((20\times 2)(2\times 1) = 20\times 1\).

    Symbols

    \(\boldsymbol\beta\)Beta: column of \(p\) parameters.
    \(\hat y_i\)Y hat: the prediction for observation \(i\). The hat means “estimated”.
    \(\hat{\mathbf y}\)Column of all \(n\) predictions.

    Example: three predictions in one product#

    A solar-panel model predicts power \(\hat y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 G + \beta_2 T\) from irradiance \(G\) (W/m²) and temperature \(T\) (°C), with \(\boldsymbol\beta = (2.0,\ 0.25,\ -0.3)\). Write the three readings below as a matrix \(\mathbf X\) (with a column of ones) and compute \(\hat{\mathbf y} = \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\).

    reading\(G\)\(T\)
    126031.1
    241035.6
    315028.4
    Solution triple-click to reveal
    \[ \mathbf X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 260 & 31.1 \\ 1 & 410 & 35.6 \\ 1 & 150 & 28.4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta = \begin{pmatrix} 2.0 + 0.25(260) - 0.3(31.1) \\ 2.0 + 0.25(410) - 0.3(35.6) \\ 2.0 + 0.25(150) - 0.3(28.4) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 57.67 \\ 93.82 \\ 30.98 \end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{W}. \]

    Each row of \(\mathbf X\) times \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is one prediction: one matrix product gives them all.
