    Multiple linear regression#

    Most outputs depend on more than one input. This page extends linear regression to many inputs, writes it in matrix form, solves it with the normal equations, and shows how categories, interactions and curved laws all fit into the same framework. The running example is a solar panel whose power depends on both sunlight and temperature.

    Many inputs#

    With \(p\) inputs the multiple linear regression model is

    \[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \dots + \beta_p X_p + \varepsilon . \]

    Geometrically it is a plane (or, with more than two inputs, a hyperplane) instead of a line. We still choose the coefficients that make the residual sum of squares, RSS, as small as possible.

    The most important sentence about multiple regression is how to read a coefficient:

    \(\beta_j\) is the change in \(Y\) when \(X_j\) increases by 1, with all the other inputs held fixed.

    Each coefficient measures the effect of one input after allowing for the others, which is exactly what an experiment does when it changes one variable at a time.

    Running example: a solar panel. We have 40 readings of the irradiance \(G\) (W/m\(^2\)), the cell temperature \(T\) (\(^\circ\)C) and the output power \(P\) (W), and fit

    \[ P = \beta_0 + \beta_1 G + \beta_2 T + \varepsilon . \]

    A panel's power rises with the sunlight falling on it, but falls as the cells get hot: silicon cells lose about 0.4% of their output per degree. The question is: how much power is lost per degree of heating? That is \(\beta_2\). The difficulty is that strong sunlight also heats the panel, so \(G\) and \(T\) change together. Multiple regression separates their effects.

    Matrix form#

    Matrices let us write all \(n\) equations at once. The facts we need, briefly:

    • A matrix is a table of numbers; “\(n \times m\)” means \(n\) rows and \(m\) columns. A vector is a single column. Bold letters (\(\mathbf X\), \(\mathbf y\), \(\boldsymbol\beta\)) denote a whole matrix or vector.
    • Matrix times vector: multiply each row by the column, entry by entry, and add. The result has one number per row. For example \[ \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&5\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1\cdot3 + 2\cdot4\\1\cdot3 + 5\cdot4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}11\\23\end{pmatrix}. \] Read it as two predictions from a line with intercept 3 and slope 4, at inputs 2 and 5.
    • The transpose \(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\) swaps rows and columns: the transpose of \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&5\end{pmatrix}\) is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&5\end{pmatrix}\).
    • The inverse \(A^{-1}\) undoes \(A\), as \(1/a\) undoes multiplication by \(a\): \(A^{-1}A = I\), where the identity matrix \(I\) has 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere and plays the role of the number 1. Only square matrices can have an inverse, and not all do (just as 0 has no inverse).

    Now put the data in a matrix, with a first column of ones for the intercept:

    \[ \mathbf X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2p}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \boldsymbol\beta = \begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\ \beta_1\\ \vdots\\ \beta_p\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf y = \begin{pmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{pmatrix}. \]

    The column of ones lets the intercept be treated like any other coefficient (\(1 \times \beta_0 = \beta_0\)). Row \(i\) of \(\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\) is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_p x_{ip}\), the model for observation \(i\). So all \(n\) equations become one:

    \[ \mathbf y = \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta + \boldsymbol\varepsilon . \]

    For the solar panel, \(\mathbf X\) has columns (ones, \(G\), \(T\)) and shape \(40 \times 3\). Its first three rows are \((1, 529.3, 35.5)\), \((1, 396.9, 31.3)\) and \((1, 910.9, 47.9)\). The third reading was taken in strong sun (911 W/m\(^2\)) and the cell is hotter (\(x_{32} = 47.9\) \(^\circ\)C).

    The normal equations#

    In matrix form \(\mathrm{RSS} = (\mathbf y - \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y - \mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)\). Setting its gradient to zero gives the normal equations, a system of \(p + 1\) linear equations for the \(p + 1\) unknown coefficients:

    \[ \mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\,\hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\beta} = \bigl(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\bigr)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y . \]

    With one input they reduce to the slope and intercept formulas of simple linear regression.

    Why “normal”? Rewrite the equations as \(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y - \mathbf X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}) = \mathbf 0\). The vector of residuals has zero dot product with every column of \(\mathbf X\): the best coefficients make the residuals perpendicular to every column. “Normal” is an old word for perpendicular. Geometrically, \(\mathbf X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) is the projection of \(\mathbf y\) onto the space spanned by the columns of \(\mathbf X\).

    In practice. In Python use np.linalg.lstsq(X, y). Do not compute the inverse yourself: it is slow and loses accuracy. Library solvers use stable methods (QR or SVD decompositions). When inputs are strongly correlated, \(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\) is nearly singular and computing its inverse magnifies rounding errors.

    This one formula fits any linear model with any number of inputs, including the dummy variables, interactions and powers introduced below: they are just more columns of \(\mathbf X\).

    Derivation and standard errors

    \(\mathrm{RSS} = \mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y - 2\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y + \boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\). Its gradient with respect to \(\boldsymbol\beta\) is \(-2\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y + 2\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\); setting it to zero gives the normal equations.

    The standard errors of the coefficients are the square roots of the diagonal entries of \(\hat\sigma^2(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\), where \(\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{RSS}/(n - p - 1)\) estimates the noise variance (ESL Section 3.2).

    Solar panel: the result#

    The fitted plane is

    \[ \hat P = 30.6 + 0.244\,G - 1.13\,T , \]

    with these estimates, standard errors (SE) and \(t\)-statistics:

    estimateSE\(t\)
    intercept30.66.84.5
    \(G\) (W per W/m\(^2\))0.2440.01220.6
    \(T\) (W per \(^\circ\)C)\(-1.13\)0.34\(-3.3\)

    The residual standard error is RSE = 7.1 W and \(R^2 = 0.980\).

    What it means. Each extra degree costs about 1.1 W, with the sunlight held fixed. Each extra 100 W/m\(^2\) of sunlight gives about \(0.244 \times 100 = 24\) W, with the temperature held fixed. Both inputs are clearly significant (\(|t| > 2\)). The practical consequence: keep panels cool, for example by mounting them with airflow behind them. The intercept has no physical meaning here (no panel produces 30 W in the dark at 0 \(^\circ\)C); it just positions the plane over the range of the data.

    The typical error, 7 W on powers of 50 to 250 W, is larger than the true measurement noise of 3 W, so something is still missing from the model. We find it below.

    Worked numbers

    Prediction for the first reading (\(G = 529.3\), \(T = 35.5\)): \(30.6 + 0.244 \times 529.3 - 1.13 \times 35.5 = 30.6 + 129.1 - 40.1 = 119.6\) W. Measured: 127.5 W; residual \(+7.9\) W.

    Warning: a coefficient's sign can flip#

    Fit the power against temperature alone and you get

    \[ \hat P = -60.2 + 5.27\,T . \]

    Each degree seems to add 5.3 W. Taken literally, we should heat our panels! With sunlight included, the temperature coefficient becomes \(-1.13\): heat costs power, as physics says.

    Why? Sunny moments are also hot moments: the correlation coefficient between \(G\) and \(T\) is \(r = 0.91\) (\(r\) runs from \(-1\) to \(1\); values near 1 mean the two rise and fall together). When \(T\) is the only input, a high \(T\) signals strong sunlight, which means high power. The simple slope mixes the true, negative effect of temperature with the positive effect of the sunlight that comes with it. This is called confounding.

    Lessons:

    • A coefficient depends on which other inputs are in the model. It answers “what happens when this input changes with the others fixed”, and “the others” are only those included.
    • Leaving out an important input can reverse a conclusion. Correlation is not causation.
    • The surest cure is an experiment: heat the panel at constant illumination in the lab.

    Qualitative inputs: dummy variables#

    Some inputs are categories: a panel is clean or dusty. A regression needs numbers, so a category with two levels is coded as a dummy variable:

    \[ D = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{dusty}\\ 0 & \text{clean} \end{cases} \qquad\qquad P = \beta_0 + \beta_1 G + \beta_2 T + \beta_3 D + \varepsilon . \]

    For a clean panel (\(D = 0\)) the model is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1 G + \beta_2 T\); for a dusty one it is the same plus \(\beta_3\). So \(\beta_3\) is the power difference, dusty minus clean, with \(G\) and \(T\) fixed.

    Solar panel (16 of the 40 readings were from a dusty panel): \(\hat\beta_3 = -11.7\) W, SE = 1.5, \(t = -8.0\). Dust costs about 12 W, and it is clearly significant. Adding it removes most of the unexplained scatter: the RSE falls from 7.1 to 4.3 W and \(R^2\) rises from 0.980 to 0.993. The temperature coefficient also becomes more precise, \(-0.67 \pm 0.21\) W/\(^\circ\)C instead of \(-1.13 \pm 0.34\). (The true value at a typical \(G = 600\) W/m\(^2\) is about \(-0.25 \times 600 \times 0.004 = -0.6\) W/\(^\circ\)C.)

    Worked numbers

    \(t = -11.67/1.45 = -8.0\). The dusty panels' average power is only 5.9 W below the clean ones' (125.7 against 131.6 W), because by chance they received more sunlight (average \(G\) 590.7 against 557.6 W/m\(^2\)). The regression compares like with like: same sunlight, same temperature.

    More than two levels. A category with \(k\) levels needs \(k - 1\) dummy variables. For panel type with three levels (monocrystalline, polycrystalline, thin film) use two dummies; the third level is the baseline, absorbed in the intercept. Using \(k\) dummies plus an intercept makes the columns of \(\mathbf X\) linearly dependent (the dummies add up to the column of ones), and the normal equations then have no unique solution.

    Example: reading a dummy variable#

    A model for the power drawn by a pump is \(\hat y = 3.2 + 0.08\,Q + 1.5\,D\) (kW), where \(Q\) is the flow (L/min) and \(D = 1\) for the old motor model and \(D = 0\) for the new one. Predict the power of each motor at \(Q = 50\) L/min, and say what 1.5 means.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    New motor (\(D = 0\)): \(3.2 + 0.08 \times 50 = 7.2\) kW. Old motor (\(D = 1\)): \(7.2 + 1.5 = 8.7\) kW.

    The coefficient 1.5 is the extra power drawn by the old motor at the same flow: the two lines are parallel, 1.5 kW apart.

    Interactions: when effects depend on each other#

    Add the product of two inputs as a new input:

    \[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \beta_3 X_1 X_2 + \varepsilon . \]

    The product \(X_1X_2\), called an interaction, is just a new column of \(\mathbf X\), so the model is still linear in the coefficients. Rewrite it as

    \[ Y = \beta_0 + (\beta_1 + \beta_3 X_2)\,X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 + \varepsilon : \]

    the slope of \(X_1\) is now \(\beta_1 + \beta_3 X_2\), which changes with \(X_2\). The effect of one input depends on the value of the other.

    Rule (the hierarchical principle): if you include \(X_1X_2\), also keep \(X_1\) and \(X_2\), even if their \(p\)-values become large, because the interaction is hard to interpret without them. In our fit, \(T\) on its own has \(p = 0.44\) once \(G \times T\) is added, but we keep it.

    Solar panel. The efficiency of a cell drops by a fraction \(\gamma\) (Greek “gamma”, about 0.004) for each degree above 25 \(^\circ\)C, so physics suggests

    \[ P \approx a\,G\,\bigl[1 - \gamma\,(T - 25)\bigr] = aG + 25a\gamma\,G - a\gamma\,GT , \]

    where \(a\) is a panel constant (W per W/m\(^2\); 0.25 in our data). The physics predicts a \(G \times T\) term: each degree costs a fixed fraction of the power, so more watts are lost in strong sun. Adding \(G \times T\) to the model gives \(t = -3.9\) for the interaction, and the RSE falls again, from 4.3 to 3.7 W, towards the true noise of 3 W.

    Worked numbers

    Fitted interaction coefficient \(-0.00145\) (SE 0.00037): \(t = -0.00145/0.00037 = -3.9\). Physics predicts \(-a\gamma = -0.25 \times 0.004 = -0.001\). The estimate is within about 1.3 SE of this.

    Curves: make them straight#

    A charged capacitor \(C\) discharging through a resistor \(R\) gives a voltage that falls exponentially with time:

    \[ V = V_0\, e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = RC , \]

    where \(V_0\) is the voltage at \(t = 0\) and \(\tau\) (Greek “tau”) is the time constant in seconds. A straight line fits this curve badly (\(R^2 = 0.82\)). But take natural logarithms of both sides, using \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\) and \(\ln e^{x} = x\):

    \[ \underbrace{\ln V}_{Y} = \underbrace{\ln V_0}_{\text{intercept}} \underbrace{-\,\tfrac{1}{\tau}}_{\text{slope}}\ \underbrace{t}_{X} . \]

    \(\ln V\) against \(t\) is a straight line. Fit it by least squares, then convert back: \(\hat\tau = -1/\text{slope}\) and \(\hat V_0 = e^{\text{intercept}}\).

    Worked numbers

    \(RC = 22\,000~\Omega \times 100 \times 10^{-6}\) F \(= 2.2\) s. Fit: slope \(-0.4551\) s\(^{-1}\), so \(\hat\tau = 1/0.4551 = 2.197\) s; intercept 1.6067, so \(\hat V_0 = e^{1.6067} = 4.99\) V (true value 5 V).

    For other curves, add columns such as \(x^2\) and \(x^3\) to \(\mathbf X\). This is polynomial regression; it is still linear in the coefficients.

    The logarithm changes the noise

    The voltage noise is 0.02 V at every point. After taking the logarithm, the error is about \(0.02/V\): small at the start (5 V) and large at the end (0.14 V). The late points therefore scatter more on the log plot and should count less. The remedy is a weighted least-squares fit, in which each squared residual is multiplied by a weight.

    Things to check#

    A fitted model can look good by its \(R^2\) and \(p\)-values and still be wrong. Always plot the residuals, against the fitted values and against each input. These are the common problems:

    ProblemWhat the residuals showFix
    Curved relationthe residuals form a curve (e.g. a U-shape)add \(x^2\), or transform a variable
    Noise grows with the output (heteroscedasticity)the residuals fan outtransform \(y\); use a weighted fit
    Outlierone very large residualcheck it; do not just delete it
    Correlated inputs (collinearity)large standard errors, strange signsdrop or combine inputs

    Unequal noise makes the standard errors wrong. An outlier may be a recording error, or the most interesting point in the data set (a sensor starting to fail). Collinearity means that when inputs move together, the data cannot separate their effects, as in the sign-flip example above. For the solar panel without the dust variable, a residual plot shows the dusty readings sitting below zero: a missing input shows up in the residuals.
