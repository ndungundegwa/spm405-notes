    Multiple linear regression#

    Many inputs: multiple linear regression#

    \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2 + \dots + \beta_pX_p + \varepsilon\)

    With \(p\) inputs the model is a plane (or hyperplane) instead of a line.

    Reading a coefficient: \(\beta_j\) = change in \(Y\) when \(X_j\) increases by 1, with all other inputs held fixed

    The most important sentence of the lecture. Each coefficient measures the effect of one input after allowing for the others. This is exactly what an experiment does when it changes one variable at a time.

    We still choose the \(\beta\)'s that make the RSS smallest

    Same criterion as before; slide 18 gives the solution.

    Warn students that ISLP uses \(p\) for both the number of inputs and the \(p\)-value; the context always tells which.

    Solar panel (40 readings): \(G\): sunlight (irradiance, W/m\(^2\)); \(T\): cell temperature (\(^\circ\)C); \(P\): output power (W). \(P = \beta_0 + \beta_1G + \beta_2T + \varepsilon\). Question: how much power is lost per degree of heating? That is \(\beta_2\)

    A solar panel's power rises with the sunlight falling on it (irradiance), but falls as the cells get hot: silicon cells lose about 0.4% of their output per degree. The difficulty: strong sunlight also heats the panel, so the two inputs change together. Multiple regression separates their effects.

    Symbols

    \(p\)Number of inputs.
    \(\beta_j\)Coefficient of input \(X_j\).
    \(G\)Irradiance: solar power per unit area (W/m\(^2\)); full sun is about 1000 W/m\(^2\).
    \(T\)Cell temperature (\(^\circ\)C).
    \(P\)Electrical output power (W).

    Reminder: vectors and matrices#

    The next two slides write the regression in matrix form. Not every student is fluent with matrices, so this slide gives exactly the facts that are used, with one worked example. Do the example at the board.

    A matrix is a table of numbers. “\(n \times m\)” means \(n\) rows and \(m\) columns. A vector is a single column of numbers. Bold letters (\(\bX\), \(\by\), \(\bb\)) mean a whole matrix or vector

    In this course the data matrix \(\bX\) has one row per reading and one column per input; \(\by\) is the column of outputs.

    Matrix times vector: multiply each row by the column, entry by entry, and add

    The result has one number per row. This is how \(\bX\bb\) gives one prediction per reading.

    Transpose \(\bX\T\): swap rows and columns. Inverse \(A^{-1}\): undoes \(A\), like \(1/a\) for numbers: \(A^{-1}A = I\)

    Only square matrices can have an inverse, and not all do (as a number has no inverse if it is 0).

    Example: \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&5\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}11\\23\end{pmatrix}\); the transpose of \(\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\1&5\end{pmatrix}\) is \(\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\2&5\end{pmatrix}\)

    The first row gives \(1 \times 3 + 2 \times 4 = 11\), the second \(1 \times 3 + 5 \times 4 = 23\). Read it as two predictions: with intercept 3 and slope 4, inputs 2 and 5 give 11 and 23. This is exactly the form \(\bX\bb\) of the next slide.

    \(I\) is the identity matrix: 1s on the diagonal, 0s elsewhere. It plays the role of the number 1

    Matrix form#

    Put the data in a matrix, with a column of ones for the intercept: \(\bX\) (\(n \times (p+1)\)), \(\bb = (\beta_0, \dots, \beta_p)\)

    Lecture 1 introduced the data matrix. Adding a first column of ones lets the intercept be treated like any other coefficient: \(1 \times \beta_0 = \beta_0\).

    Then all \(n\) equations become one: \(\by = \bX\bb + \boldsymbol\varepsilon\). One row of \(\bX\) = one observation

    Row \(i\) of \(\bX\bb\) is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_px_{ip}\), the model for observation \(i\). The \(n\) separate equations are written as one matrix equation.

    Solar panel: \(\bX\) with rows \((1, 529.3, 35.5)\), \((1, 396.9, 31.3)\), \((1, 910.9, 47.9)\), …. Columns: ones, \(G\), \(T\). Shape: \(40 \times 3\)

    The first three of the 40 readings. The third was taken in strong sun (911 W/m\(^2\)), and the cell is hotter (47.9 \(^\circ\)C).

    Two subscripts: the first is the row (which reading), the second the column (which input). \(x_{32} = 47.9\) \(^\circ\)C here.

    Symbols

    \(\bX\)Data matrix with a first column of ones.
    \(\bb\)Column of coefficients \(\beta_0, \dots, \beta_p\).
    \(\by\)Column of the \(n\) outputs.
    \(\boldsymbol\varepsilon\)Column of the \(n\) errors.

    The normal equations#

    Minimising the RSS gives one equation for all the coefficients: \(\bX\T\bX\,\hat\bb = \bX\T\by\), so \(\hat\bb = (\bX\T\bX)^{-1}\bX\T\by\)

    In matrix form \(\mathrm{RSS} = (\by - \bX\bb)\T(\by - \bX\bb)\). Setting its gradient to zero gives the normal equations: a system of \(p + 1\) linear equations for \(p + 1\) unknowns. With one input they reduce to the formulas of slide 9.

    In words: the best coefficients make the residuals perpendicular (“normal”) to every column of \(\bX\)

    Rewrite as \(\bX\T(\by - \bX\hat\bb) = \mathbf 0\): the residual vector has zero dot product with every column of \(\bX\). “Normal” is an old word for perpendicular. Geometrically, \(\bX\hat\bb\) is the projection of \(\by\) onto the space spanned by the columns.

    In Python: np.linalg.lstsq(X, y). Do not compute the inverse yourself: it is slow and loses accuracy

    Library solvers use stable methods (QR or SVD decompositions). When inputs are strongly correlated, \(\bX\T\bX\) is nearly singular, and computing its inverse magnifies rounding errors.

    One line of code: this formula fits any linear model, with any number of inputs

    Including dummy variables, interactions and powers (slides 21–23): they are just more columns of \(\bX\).

    Symbols

    \(\bX\T\)Transpose of \(\bX\).
    \((\cdot)^{-1}\)Matrix inverse.
    \(\hat\bb\)Least-squares estimate of all coefficients.
    Beyond the slide: derivation and standard errors (ESL 3.2)

    \(\mathrm{RSS} = \by\T\by - 2\bb\T\bX\T\by + \bb\T\bX\T\bX\bb\). Its gradient is \(-2\bX\T\by + 2\bX\T\bX\bb\); setting it to zero gives the normal equations. The standard errors are the square roots of the diagonal of \(\hat\sigma^2(\bX\T\bX)^{-1}\), with \(\hat\sigma^2 = \mathrm{RSS}/(n - p - 1)\). The notebook's fit function computes exactly this.

    Solar panel: the result#

    valueSE\(t\)
    intercept30.66.84.5
    \(G\)0.2440.01220.6
    \(T\)\(-1.13\)0.34\(-3.3\)

    \(\hat P = 30.6 + 0.244\,G - 1.13\,T\)

    The fitted plane.

    Table: intercept 30.6 (SE 6.8, \(t = 4.5\)); \(G\) 0.244 (SE 0.012, \(t = 20.6\)); \(T\) \(-1.13\) (SE 0.34, \(t = -3.3\))

    Both inputs are clearly significant (\(\vert t\vert > 2\)). The intercept has no physical meaning here (no panel produces 30 W in the dark at 0 \(^\circ\)C); it just positions the plane over the range of the data.

    RSE = 7.1 W, \(R^2 = 0.980\)

    The typical error is 7 W on powers of 50 to 250 W. The true noise is 3 W, so something is still missing: dust (slide 21).

    What it means: each extra degree costs about 1.1 W (sunlight held fixed). Each extra 100 W/m\(^2\) of sunlight gives about 24 W (temperature held fixed). So: keep panels cool

    \(0.244 \times 100 = 24.4\) W per 100 W/m\(^2\). The temperature coefficient is the answer to the question on slide 15. Practical consequence: mount panels with airflow behind them.

    Worked numbers

    Prediction for the first reading (\(G = 529.3\), \(T = 35.5\)): \(30.6 + 0.244 \times 529.3 - 1.13 \times 35.5 = 30.6 + 129.1 - 40.1 = 119.6\) W. Measured: 127.5 W; residual \(+7.9\) W.

    Warning: the sign can flip#

    Power against temperature alone: \(\hat P = -60.2 + 5.27\,T\). Heat gives more power?

    Fitting \(P\) against \(T\) only gives a positive slope: each degree seems to add 5.3 W. Taken literally, we should heat our panels.

    With sunlight included: \(\hat P = 30.6 + 0.244\,G - 1.13\,T\). Now heat gives less power

    With \(G\) in the model, the temperature coefficient becomes negative, as physics says.

    Why? Sunny moments are also hot moments (\(r = 0.91\)). Alone, \(T\) takes the credit for the sunlight

    The correlation between \(G\) and \(T\) is 0.91. When \(T\) is the only input, high \(T\) signals strong sunlight, which means high power. The simple slope mixes the true (negative) effect of temperature with the (positive) effect of the sunlight that comes with it. This is called confounding.

    Lessons: a coefficient depends on the other inputs. Correlation is not causation. Where you can: do the experiment

    A coefficient answers “what happens when this input changes with the others fixed”, and “the others” are only those in the model. Leaving out an important input can reverse a conclusion. The surest cure is an experiment: heat the panel at constant illumination in the lab.

    Symbols

    \(r\)Correlation coefficient, between \(-1\) and \(1\).

    Qualitative inputs: dummy variables#

    Some inputs are categories, e.g. a panel is clean or dusty. Code it as a dummy variable: \(D = 1\) dusty, \(D = 0\) clean

    A regression needs numbers. A category with two levels becomes a 0/1 variable (ISLP Section 3.3.1).

    \(P = \beta_0 + \beta_1G + \beta_2T + \beta_3D + \varepsilon\). Symbols: \(D\): dummy (0/1) variable, 1 for a dusty panel; \(\beta_3\): power difference, dusty minus clean, with \(G\) and \(T\) fixed (W)

    For a clean panel (\(D = 0\)) the model is \(\beta_0 + \beta_1G + \beta_2T\); for a dusty one it is the same plus \(\beta_3\). So \(\beta_3\) is the shift in power caused by dust.

    Solar panel (16 of 40 dusty): \(\hat\beta_3 = -11.7\) W, SE = 1.5, \(t = -8.0\). Dust costs about 12 W. RSE: \(7.1 \to 4.3\) W

    Dust is clearly significant (\(t = -8\)). Adding it removes most of the unexplained scatter: the RSE falls from 7.1 to 4.3 W, and \(R^2\) rises from 0.980 to 0.993. The temperature coefficient also becomes more precise: \(-0.67 \pm 0.21\) W/\(^\circ\)C (it was \(-1.13 \pm 0.34\)). The true value at a typical \(G = 600\) W/m\(^2\) is about \(-0.25 \times 600 \times 0.004 = -0.6\) W/\(^\circ\)C.

    More than two levels: \(k\) categories need \(k - 1\) dummy variables

    For panel type with three levels (monocrystalline, polycrystalline, thin film), use two dummies; the third level is the baseline, absorbed in the intercept. Using \(k\) dummies plus an intercept makes the columns of \(\bX\) linearly dependent, and the normal equations have no unique solution.

    Symbols

    \(D\)Dummy variable: 1 for dusty, 0 for clean.
    \(\beta_3\)Power difference, dusty minus clean (W).
    \(k\)Number of levels (categories) of a qualitative input.

    Worked numbers

    \(t = -11.67/1.45 = -8.0\). The dusty panels' average power is only 5.9 W below the clean ones' (125.7 vs 131.6 W), because by chance they received more sunlight (average \(G\) 590.7 against 557.6 W/m\(^2\)); the regression compares like with like (notebook question 8).

    Example: reading a dummy variable#

    A model for the power drawn by a pump is \(\hat y = 3.2 + 0.08\,Q + 1.5\,D\) (kW), where \(Q\) is the flow (L/min) and \(D = 1\) for the old motor model and \(D = 0\) for the new one. Predict the power of each motor at \(Q = 50\) L/min, and say what 1.5 means.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    New motor (\(D = 0\)): \(3.2 + 0.08 \times 50 = 7.2\) kW. Old motor (\(D = 1\)): \(7.2 + 1.5 = 8.7\) kW.

    The coefficient 1.5 is the extra power drawn by the old motor at the same flow: the two lines are parallel, 1.5 kW apart.

    Interactions: when effects depend on each other#

    Add the product of two inputs: \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2 + \beta_3X_1X_2\)

    The product \(X_1X_2\) is a new column of \(\bX\), so the model is still linear in the coefficients.

    Now the effect of \(X_1\) depends on \(X_2\)

    Rewrite: \(Y = \beta_0 + (\beta_1 + \beta_3X_2)X_1 + \beta_2X_2\). The slope of \(X_1\) is \(\beta_1 + \beta_3X_2\), which changes with \(X_2\).

    Rule: if you include \(X_1X_2\), also keep \(X_1\) and \(X_2\)

    The hierarchical principle (ISLP 3.3.2): keep the main effects even if their \(p\)-values become large, because the interaction is hard to interpret without them. In our fit, \(T\) alone has \(p = 0.44\) once \(G \times T\) is added, but we keep it.

    Solar panel: efficiency drops about 0.4% per \(^\circ\)C, so \(P \approx a\,G\,[1 - \gamma(T - 25)]\). This contains a \(G \times T\) term: heat costs more power in strong sun

    Multiply out: \(P \approx aG + 25a\gamma G - a\gamma\,GT\). The physics predicts an interaction: each degree costs a fixed fraction of the power, so more watts in strong sun.

    Adding \(G \times T\): \(t = -3.9\); RSE \(4.3 \to 3.7\) W

    The interaction is significant and reduces the error again, towards the true noise of 3 W.

    Symbols

    \(X_1X_2\)Interaction: the product of two inputs.
    \(a\)Panel constant (W per W/m\(^2\); 0.25 in the simulation).
    \(\gamma\)Temperature coefficient of efficiency (0.004 per \(^\circ\)C).

    Worked numbers

    Fitted interaction coefficient \(-0.00145\) (SE 0.00037): \(t = -0.00145/0.00037 = -3.9\). Physics: \(-a\gamma = -0.25 \times 0.004 = -0.001\) (times \(1 - 0.08D\)). The estimate is within about 1.3 SE of this.

    Curves: make them straight#

    Capacitor discharge is a curve: \(V = V_0e^{-t/\tau}\), \(\tau = RC\)

    A charged capacitor discharging through a resistor: the voltage falls exponentially with time constant \(\tau = RC\). A straight line fits this badly (notebook: \(R^2 = 0.82\)).

    Take logs: a straight line! \(\ln V = \ln V_0 - (1/\tau)\,t\)

    \(\ln V\) against \(t\) is a straight line with intercept \(\ln V_0\) and slope \(-1/\tau\). Fit it by least squares and convert back.

    The last rule is what makes the trick work: \(\ln(V_0e^{-t/\tau}) = \ln V_0 - t/\tau\).

    Other curves: add columns such as \(x^2\) to \(\bX\)

    Polynomial regression (ISLP 3.3.2): columns \(x\), \(x^2\), \(x^3\). Still linear in the coefficients (slide 6).

    Plot and result: \(R = 22\) k\(\Omega\), \(C = 100~\mu\)F. Slope \(-0.455\) s\(^{-1}\), so \(\hat\tau = 1/0.455 = 2.20\) s. True \(RC = 2.2\) s

    The 17 points of \(\ln V\) fall on a straight line. The fitted time constant matches the component values.

    Symbols

    \(V_0\)Voltage at \(t = 0\) (5 V).
    \(\tau\)Time constant (s).
    \(R\), \(C\)Resistance (\(\Omega\)) and capacitance (F).
    \(\ln\)Natural logarithm.

    Worked numbers

    \(RC = 22\,000~\Omega \times 100 \times 10^{-6}\) F \(= 2.2\) s. Fit: slope \(-0.4551\) s\(^{-1}\), so \(\hat\tau = 1/0.4551 = 2.197\) s; intercept 1.6067, so \(\hat V_0 = e^{1.6067} = 4.99\) V.

    Beyond the slide: the log changes the noise

    The noise is 0.02 V at every point. After the log, the error is about \(0.02/V\): small at the start (5 V) and large at the end (0.14 V). The late points therefore scatter more on the log plot and should count less: a weighted fit (notebook question 9).
