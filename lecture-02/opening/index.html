    From last week: your group problems#

    PartLinear regression
    Representationa straight line (or plane)
    Evaluationsum of squared errors
    Optimisationa formula, or gradient descent

    Every ML method has three parts. For linear regression: representation — a straight line (or plane); evaluation — sum of squared errors; optimisation — a formula, or gradient descent

    Lecture 1 described every ML method by three choices. Linear regression is the simplest example of each: the model family is all straight lines (or planes, with several inputs); a line is scored by the sum of its squared errors; and the best line is found either exactly by a formula (slides 9 and 18) or step by step by gradient descent (slides 25–29).

    Group presentations (15 minutes): one minute per group: the problem your group will solve. What is \(X\)? What is \(Y\)? Regression or classification? Where will the data come from? The class critiques each choice

    Each group states its project problem in the language of Lecture 1: the inputs \(X\) (what is measured), the output \(Y\) (what is predicted), whether \(Y\) is a number (regression) or a category (classification), and where the data will come from. Other groups question each choice.

    Try it: Keep strictly to one minute per group. Note problems where the data source is unclear; those groups need to find data first (slide 32).

    This lecture is the first with real formulas, and the same few words and letters appear on almost every slide from now on. Students who skipped Lecture 1, or who learnt statistics with other notation, should meet them once, with an example, before they are used. Go through the table quickly, pointing at the sensor column.

    \(X\) (input): what we measure and use to predict; example: reference temperature

    Also called a predictor, a feature or an independent variable. In the calibration, the temperature read from a trusted reference thermometer.

    \(Y\) (output): what we want to predict; example: sensor voltage

    Also called the response, target or dependent variable.

    Data point: one measured example (also: observation, reading); example: one reading

    One row of the data table: one temperature and the voltage measured at it.

    \(i\): numbers the data points, \(i = 1, 2, \dots, n\); \(n\): how many data points we have, \(n = 20\)

    \(i\) is a label, not a quantity: reading 1, reading 2, and so on.

    \(x_i\), \(y_i\): input and output of data point \(i\)

    \(x_7\) is the temperature of the seventh reading and \(y_7\) its voltage.

    Capital \(X\), \(Y\): the quantities in general. Small \(x_i\), \(y_i\): one particular measured value

    “\(Y\) rises with \(X\)” is a statement about temperature and voltage in general; “\(y_7 = 380\) mV” is one number in the table.

    Three marks on symbols: hat, bar and sum#

    MarkMeaningExample
    hat: \(\hat y\)predicted or estimated from data\(\hat y_i\) = the voltage our line predicts for reading \(i\)
    bar: \(\bar x\)the average\(\bar x = (x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n)/n\)
    sum: \(\sum_i\)add up over all data points\(\sum_i x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n\)

    Hat: \(\hat y\): predicted or estimated from data; example: \(\hat y_i\) = the voltage our line predicts for reading \(i\)

    The hat is the most important mark of the course. \(y_i\) is what was measured; \(\hat y_i\) is what the model says. Their difference is the error the model makes on that point. A hat on a coefficient, \(\hat\beta_1\), means the value estimated from our data, as opposed to the unknown true value \(\beta_1\).

    Bar: \(\bar x\): the average; \(\bar x = (x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n)/n\)

    Pronounced “x bar”.

    Sum: \(\sum_i\): add up over all data points; \(\sum_i x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n\)

    The capital Greek letter sigma, written large. The \(i\) underneath says what is being counted.

    Example with 3 numbers: \(x_1 = 2\), \(x_2 = 4\), \(x_3 = 9\): \(\sum_i x_i = 2 + 4 + 9 = 15\); \(\bar x = 15/3 = 5\)

    Try it: Ask a student to compute \(\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)\) for these numbers: \((-3) + (-1) + 4 = 0\). Deviations from the average always add up to zero, which is why the formulas square them.
