    Simple linear regression#

    What is linear regression?#

    We have inputs \(X\) and a numerical output \(Y\)

    Linear regression is a supervised method for regression problems: the output is a number.

    We assume \(Y\) changes in a straight line with \(X\)

    With one input: each extra unit of \(X\) changes \(Y\) by the same amount, wherever we are on the line.

    Real relationships are rarely exactly straight, but often close enough

    Over a limited range, many relations are nearly straight. A linear model is simple, fast and easy to interpret, and often predicts well.

    The coefficients \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots\) (“beta”) are the numbers the model learns from the data

    A model has a fixed form, chosen by us, and some numbers left open. Fitting the model means finding those numbers from the data. \(\beta\) is the Greek letter beta; the small number underneath only counts the coefficients.

    “Linear” means linear in the coefficients. A curve such as \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X + \beta_2X^2\) still counts

    The word “linear” refers to how the unknown coefficients appear, not to the shape of the graph. \(\beta_0 + \beta_1X + \beta_2X^2\) is a parabola in \(X\), but it is a sum of coefficients times known numbers (\(1\), \(X\), \(X^2\)), so the same method fits it. Slide 23 uses this.

    “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” — George Box

    George Box was a British statistician. No model captures reality exactly; the question is whether it is good enough for the purpose.

    You already fit straight lines in the lab (Ohm's law, sensor calibration). Today we do it properly, and we find the uncertainty of the result

    Students have drawn best-fit lines by eye or with a calculator. Today: the exact method (least squares), and how to say how sure we are of the slope and intercept.

    Symbols

    \(X\), \(Y\)Input and output.
    \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2\)Coefficients (unknown numbers) of the model.

    The model with one input#

    \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1X + \varepsilon\): output = intercept + slope \(\times\) input + random error

    The simple linear regression model. \(\beta_0\) is the value of \(Y\) when \(X = 0\); \(\beta_1\) is the change in \(Y\) for each unit increase of \(X\); \(\varepsilon\) is everything the straight line does not capture (noise, unmeasured effects), with mean zero.

    \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\) are unknown. From data we compute estimates \(\hat\beta_0\), \(\hat\beta_1\) and predict \(\hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1x\)

    The true coefficients are never known exactly; we estimate them from \(n\) data points. Once estimated, a prediction for any new input \(x\) is \(\hat y\).

    The Symbols box reads the equation in words. Note that \(\varepsilon\) is not a mistake in the model but the scatter of real measurements: two readings at the same temperature never give exactly the same voltage.

    Calibrating a temperature sensor: an analogue sensor gives a voltage \(V\) that rises with temperature \(T\): \(V = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T\). \(\beta_1\): sensitivity (mV/\(^\circ\)C); \(\beta_0\): offset (mV). Data: 20 readings against a reference thermometer. Datasheet: 10 mV/\(^\circ\)C, no offset

    Calibration is a daily task in instrumentation, and it is a linear regression. Sensors such as the LM35 give an output voltage proportional to temperature. Here the input is the true temperature from a trusted reference thermometer, and the output is the sensor voltage. The slope is the sensitivity: millivolts per degree. The intercept is the offset: the output at 0 \(^\circ\)C. The datasheet promises 10 mV/\(^\circ\)C and zero offset. Our job: measure both, with their uncertainties, and check the datasheet.

    Symbols

    \(\varepsilon\)Random error, mean zero.
    \(\hat\beta_0\), \(\hat\beta_1\)Estimates of the intercept and slope.
    \(\hat y\)Predicted output for input \(x\).
    \(V\)Sensor output voltage (mV).
    \(T\)Temperature from the reference thermometer (\(^\circ\)C).

    Least squares: what is the “best” line?#

    Residual = measured \(-\) predicted: \(e_i = y_i - \hat y_i\)

    For each data point, the residual is the vertical distance from the point to the line: positive if the point is above the line, negative if below.

    Residual sum of squares: \(\mathrm{RSS} = e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \dots + e_n^2\)

    Square each residual (so that positive and negative ones do not cancel, and large ones count heavily) and add them up.

    The best line has the smallest RSS: least squares

    Of all possible straight lines, choose the one with the smallest RSS. This is the “evaluation” part of slide 3.

    Plot: 20 calibration readings and the least-squares line

    The readings lie very close to a straight line: the sensor is linear, and the noise (about 15 mV) is small compared with the range of the output (60 to 920 mV).

    Without squaring, a line far above some points and far below others could have residuals that add to zero. Squares are always positive.

    Symbols

    \(e_i\)Residual of point \(i\) (units of \(y\), here mV).
    \(y_i\), \(\hat y_i\)Measured and predicted output of point \(i\).
    RSSResidual sum of squares (units of \(y\) squared, mV\(^2\)).
    \(n\)Number of data points (here 20).

    The least-squares line#

    Set the derivatives of the RSS to zero and solve: \(\hat\beta_1 = \sum_i(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) / \sum_i(x_i - \bar x)^2\), \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\bar x\)

    The RSS is a function of the two unknowns \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\). At its minimum both partial derivatives are zero. Solving these two equations gives the two formulas. The second formula says the least-squares line always passes through the point of averages \((\bar x, \bar y)\).

    In words: the slope is how \(x\) and \(y\) vary together, divided by how much \(x\) varies

    The numerator is large and positive when points with \(x\) above average also have \(y\) above average. The denominator measures the spread of \(x\).

    Your turn (5 minutes): differentiate \(\mathrm{RSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1x_i)^2\) with respect to \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), set both to zero, and derive the two formulas

    A short derivation that students do in pairs; all it needs is the chain rule.

    These are the marks of slide 5, now used together.

    Beyond the slide: the derivation

    \(\partial\mathrm{RSS}/\partial\beta_0 = -2\sum_i (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1x_i) = 0\) gives \(n\beta_0 = \sum y_i - \beta_1\sum x_i\), so \(\beta_0 = \bar y - \beta_1\bar x\).

    \(\partial\mathrm{RSS}/\partial\beta_1 = -2\sum_i x_i(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1x_i) = 0\). Substitute \(\beta_0\): \(\sum_i x_i\,[(y_i - \bar y) - \beta_1(x_i - \bar x)] = 0\). Since \(\sum_i \bar x (y_i - \bar y) = 0\) and \(\sum_i \bar x (x_i - \bar x) = 0\), \(x_i\) can be replaced by \(x_i - \bar x\), giving \(\beta_1 = \sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) / \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2\).

    Symbols

    \(\bar x\), \(\bar y\)Averages of the inputs and of the outputs.
    \(\sum_i\)Sum over all data points \(i = 1, \dots, n\).

    Calibration: the result#

    \(\hat V = 2.5 + 9.83\,T\)

    The least-squares line for the 20 readings.

    Sensitivity: \(\hat\beta_1 = 9.83\) mV/\(^\circ\)C

    Close to the datasheet value of 10. Whether 9.83 is consistent with 10 is the question of slide 11.

    Offset: \(\hat\beta_0 = 2.5\) mV. The datasheet says 0. Is 2.5 a real offset?

    The estimate is not exactly zero, but every estimate from noisy data differs a little from the truth. Slide 12 tests it.

    Using the calibration: the sensor reads \(V = 500\) mV. Invert the line: \(T = (V - \hat\beta_0)/\hat\beta_1 = (500 - 2.5)/9.83 \approx 50.6~^\circ\)C

    The purpose of calibration: turn a raw sensor reading into a temperature. With the datasheet formula (\(T = V/10\)) the answer would be 50.0 \(^\circ\)C; the calibration corrects it by 0.6 \(^\circ\)C.

    From the data: \(\bar T = 47.7~^\circ\)C, \(\bar V = 471.6\) mV

    The averages used in the formulas of slide 9.

    Worked numbers

    From the notebook: \(\sum(T_i - \bar T)(V_i - \bar V) = 154\,992.3\) and \(\sum(T_i - \bar T)^2 = 15\,764.0\).

    \(\hat\beta_1 = 154\,992.3 / 15\,764.0 = 9.832\) mV/\(^\circ\)C.

    \(\hat\beta_0 = 471.64 - 9.832 \times 47.71 = 471.64 - 469.09 = 2.55 \approx 2.5\) mV (2.54 with unrounded averages).

    Inversion: \((500 - 2.54)/9.832 = 497.46/9.832 = 50.6~^\circ\)C.

    Example: calibrating a pressure sensor#

    Five readings give the sensor voltage \(V\) (mV) at reference pressures \(P\) (kPa):

    \(P\)0255075100
    \(V\)2.151.8101.0150.6199.5

    Find the least-squares line \(\hat V = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 P\), then the pressure for a reading of \(V = 120\) mV.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Averages: \(\bar P = 50\) kPa, \(\bar V = 101.0\) mV.

    \[ \sum_i (P_i - \bar P)(V_i - \bar V) = 12340, \qquad \sum_i (P_i - \bar P)^2 = 6250 \]

    so \(\hat\beta_1 = 12340/6250 = 1.9744\) mV/kPa and \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar V - \hat\beta_1\bar P = 101.0 - 1.9744 \times 50 = 2.28\) mV.

    For \(V = 120\) mV: \(P = (120 - 2.28)/1.9744 = 59.6\) kPa.

    How sure are we? Standard error#

    20 other readings would give a slightly different slope. The standard error (SE) measures this spread

    If we repeated the calibration with 20 new readings, the noise would be different and so would the slope. The SE is the standard deviation of the slope over many such repetitions. It is the “\(\pm\)” of the result.

    \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = \sigma / \sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}\): noise divided by the spread of \(x\)

    The slope is uncertain when the noise \(\sigma\) is large, and well determined when the \(x\) values are spread out. In practice \(\sigma\) is unknown and is replaced by its estimate, the RSE (slide 14).

    95% confidence interval: \(\hat\beta_1 \pm 2\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\)

    An interval built this way contains the true value in about 95% of repetitions. (The exact multiplier is 2.10 for 18 degrees of freedom; 2 is the usual rounded value.)

    The second line is the honest meaning of “95%”: it is a property of the method, over many repeats, not a probability for this one interval.

    Calibration data: SE = 0.13 mV/\(^\circ\)C; \(\beta_1 = 9.83 \pm 0.13\) mV/\(^\circ\)C; 95% interval \([9.58, 10.09]\). The datasheet value, 10, is inside

    The measured sensitivity is consistent with the datasheet: 9.83 is only \(0.17/0.13 = 1.3\) standard errors below 10.

    Smaller SE: less noise; more data; wider range of \(x\)

    Three ways to a more precise calibration: a quieter sensor or averaging, more readings, and readings spread over the whole working range. Notebook questions 3 and 4 show the effects.

    Symbols

    SEStandard error: the standard deviation of an estimate over repeated experiments.
    \(\sigma\)Standard deviation of the noise \(\varepsilon\) (here truly 15 mV).

    Worked numbers

    \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = \mathrm{RSE}/\sqrt{15\,764.0} = 15.92/125.55 = 0.127\) mV/\(^\circ\)C.

    Interval: \(9.832 - 2 \times 0.127 = 9.58\); \(9.832 + 2 \times 0.127 = 10.09\).

    Beyond the slide: SE of the intercept

    \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)^2 = \sigma^2\,[1/n + \bar x^2/\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2]\). Here \(15.92^2 \times (1/20 + 47.71^2/15\,764.0) = 253.4 \times (0.05 + 0.1444) = 49.3\), so \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0) = 7.0\) mV (slide 12). The intercept is uncertain because the data are far from \(T = 0\).

    Is the effect real? The \(t\)-statistic#

    Test the idea “no effect”: is the true coefficient 0? \(t\) = estimate / standard error \(= \hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)\)

    The \(t\)-statistic measures how many standard errors the estimate is away from zero.

    \(t\) counts how many standard errors the estimate is away from 0. \(\vert t\vert \) large (more than about 2): the effect is real. \(\vert t\vert \) small: the estimate could be 0 plus noise

    If the true value were zero, an estimate more than about 2 SE away would be rare: the 95% interval of slide 11 would not contain 0.

    Calibration data: sensitivity \(t = 9.83/0.13 = 77.5\); offset \(t = 2.5/7.0 = 0.36\). The sensitivity is 77 standard errors from 0. The offset is less than one

    The two numbers show the two outcomes: one clearly real, one indistinguishable from zero.

    A negative \(t\) is just as strong evidence as a positive one; only the size matters. “Null hypothesis” is the name statistics books use for the “no effect” idea.

    Worked numbers

    Offset: \(t = 2.54/7.02 = 0.36\). Sensitivity: \(t = 9.832/0.1268 = 77.5\).

    Example: the t-statistic of the sensitivity#

    The calibration gives the sensitivity \(\hat\beta_1 = 9.83\) mV/°C with standard error \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 0.12\) mV/°C. (a) Test \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\). (b) Is the datasheet value of 10 mV/°C consistent with the data?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    (a) \(t = \hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE} = 9.83/0.12 = 81.9\). This is enormous (anything beyond about 2 is significant at 5%): the sensor certainly responds to temperature.

    (b) Test against 10 instead of 0: \(t = (9.83 - 10)/0.12 = -1.42\). \(|t| < 2\), so the difference from the datasheet is within the noise. A 95% interval is \(9.83 \pm 2 \times 0.12 = [9.59, 10.07]\), which contains 10.

    The \(p\)-value#

    valueSE\(t\)\(p\)
    offset2.57.00.360.72
    sensitivity9.830.1377.5\(\approx 0\)

    \(p\)-value: if there were no effect, the chance of getting a \(\vert t\vert \) at least this large from noise alone

    Computed from the \(t\) distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom. A small \(p\) means the data are hard to explain without the effect.

    Small \(p\): noise alone rarely does this, so the effect is probably real. \(p < 0.05\): we call the effect significant

    A convention, not a law of nature.

    Careful: \(p\) is not the chance that the effect is zero. A large \(p\) means “no evidence”, not “no effect”

    The most common misreading. “Not significant” means “the data cannot tell it apart from zero”, not “it is zero”. With only three readings almost nothing is significant, however real.

    Table: offset 2.5, SE 7.0, \(t = 0.36\), \(p = 0.72\); sensitivity 9.83, SE 0.13, \(t = 77.5\), \(p \approx 0\)

    The sensitivity is 77 standard errors from zero: certainly real (of course; the sensor responds to temperature). The offset is only 0.36 standard errors from zero: an offset of 2.5 mV is exactly what noise alone would often produce.

    Sensitivity: certainly real. Offset: could be zero, as the datasheet says

    Conclusion of the calibration: the sensor meets its datasheet within the uncertainty.

    Symbols

    \(t\)Estimate divided by its standard error.
    \(p\)Probability of \(\vert t\vert \) at least this large if the true coefficient were 0.

    How good is the fit? RSE and \(R^2\)#

    Residual standard error: the typical size of a residual. \(\mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-2)}\)

    The RSE estimates the noise \(\sigma\). We divide by \(n - 2\), not \(n\), because two coefficients were estimated from the same data, which makes the residuals slightly smaller than the true errors.

    \(R^2\): the fraction of the variation in \(Y\) that the model explains. \(R^2 = 1 - \mathrm{RSS}/\mathrm{TSS}\), \(\mathrm{TSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2\)

    TSS (total sum of squares) is the variation of \(y\) around its average, before using \(x\). RSS is what is left after the fit. Their ratio is the fraction unexplained.

    \(R^2 = 0\): explains nothing. \(R^2 = 1\): perfect

    \(R^2\) has no units, which makes it easy to compare between problems.

    Calibration data: RSE = 15.9 mV, about 1.6 \(^\circ\)C. \(R^2 = 0.997\): temperature explains 99.7% of the variation in the output

    The RSE is close to the true noise, 15 mV. Divided by the sensitivity, \(15.9/9.83 = 1.6~^\circ\)C: a single reading of this sensor is uncertain by about \(\pm1.6~^\circ\)C. \(R^2 = 0.997\): temperature explains 99.7% of the variation in the output.

    A “good” \(R^2\) depends on the field: a calibration expects more than 0.99

    A calibration with \(R^2 = 0.9\) would mean a faulty sensor. A model of household electricity use from survey data with \(R^2 = 0.5\) may be excellent, because people are unpredictable.

    Symbols

    RSEResidual standard error (units of \(y\)).
    TSSTotal sum of squares, \(\sum(y_i - \bar y)^2\).
    \(R^2\)Fraction of the variation explained, between 0 and 1.

    Worked numbers

    \(\mathrm{RSS} = \mathrm{RSE}^2 \times (n - 2) = 15.92^2 \times 18 = 4562\) mV\(^2\). \(\mathrm{TSS} = 1\,528\,457\) mV\(^2\), so \(R^2 = 1 - 4562/1\,528\,457 = 1 - 0.0030 = 0.997\).

    Example: RSE and R² from the sums#

    A fit to \(n = 20\) calibration readings has \(\mathrm{RSS} = 4480\) mV² and total sum of squares \(\mathrm{TSS} = \sum(y_i - \bar y)^2 = 1\,515\,000\) mV². Compute the residual standard error and \(R^2\).

    Solution triple-click to reveal
    \[ \mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-2}} = \sqrt{\frac{4480}{18}} = 15.8\ \mathrm{mV}, \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{4480}{1\,515\,000} = 0.9970. \]

    A typical reading lies about 15.8 mV from the line, and the line explains 99.70% of the variation in the voltage.
