    Simple linear regression#

    Linear regression is the simplest supervised method for predicting a number, and the foundation for much of what follows. This page fits a straight line to data by least squares, using the calibration of a temperature sensor as the running example, and then asks how far the result can be trusted: standard errors, \(t\)-statistics, \(p\)-values, and \(R^2\).

    In the language of Lecture 1, linear regression is the simplest example of each ingredient:

    IngredientLinear regression
    Representationa straight line (or, with several inputs, a plane)
    Evaluationthe sum of the squared errors
    Optimisationan exact formula, or gradient descent

    Three marks on symbols: hat, bar and sum#

    Three marks appear in nearly every formula from now on.

    MarkMeaningExample
    hat: \(\hat y\)predicted or estimated from data\(\hat y_i\) = the value our line predicts for data point \(i\)
    bar: \(\bar x\)the average\(\bar x = (x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n)/n\)
    sum: \(\sum_i\)add up over all data points\(\sum_i x_i = x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n\)

    The hat is the most important mark of the course. \(y_i\) is what was measured; \(\hat y_i\) is what the model says. Their difference is the error the model makes on that point. A hat on a coefficient, \(\hat\beta_1\), means the value estimated from our data, as opposed to the unknown true value \(\beta_1\).

    Example. For \(x_1 = 2\), \(x_2 = 4\), \(x_3 = 9\): \(\sum_i x_i = 2 + 4 + 9 = 15\) and \(\bar x = 15/3 = 5\). The deviations from the average are \(-3, -1, +4\), and they add up to zero. Deviations from the average always add up to zero, which is why the formulas below square them.

    What is linear regression?#

    Linear regression assumes that the output \(Y\) changes in a straight line with the input \(X\): each extra unit of \(X\) changes \(Y\) by the same amount, wherever we are on the line. Real relationships are rarely exactly straight, but over a limited range many are close enough. A linear model is simple, fast and easy to interpret, and it often predicts well.

    The model has a fixed form, chosen by us, and some numbers left open, the coefficients \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots\) (Greek “beta”; the subscript only counts them). Fitting the model means finding those numbers from the data.

    “Linear” means linear in the coefficients, not that the graph is a straight line. The curve \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \beta_2 X^2\) is a parabola in \(X\), but it is still a sum of coefficients times known numbers (\(1\), \(X\), \(X^2\)), so the same method fits it. We use this on the next page.

    As the statistician George Box put it, “All models are wrong, but some are useful.” No model captures reality exactly; the question is whether it is good enough for the purpose.

    The model with one input#

    The simple linear regression model is

    \[ Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \varepsilon , \]

    output = intercept + slope \(\times\) input + random error. Here \(\beta_0\), the intercept, is the value of \(Y\) when \(X = 0\); \(\beta_1\), the slope, is the change in \(Y\) for each unit increase of \(X\); and \(\varepsilon\) is everything the straight line does not capture (noise, unmeasured effects), with average zero. \(\varepsilon\) is not a mistake in the model but the scatter of real measurements: two readings at the same temperature never give exactly the same voltage.

    The true coefficients are never known exactly. From \(n\) data points we compute estimates \(\hat\beta_0\), \(\hat\beta_1\), and then predict the output for any input \(x\) as

    \[ \hat y = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 x . \]

    Running example: calibrating a temperature sensor. Analogue temperature sensors such as the LM35 give an output voltage \(V\) that rises with temperature \(T\):

    \[ V = \beta_0 + \beta_1 T . \]

    The slope \(\beta_1\) is the sensitivity in mV per \(^\circ\)C; the intercept \(\beta_0\) is the offset, the output at 0 \(^\circ\)C, in mV. We take \(n = 20\) readings, each pairing the sensor voltage with the true temperature from a trusted reference thermometer. The datasheet promises a sensitivity of 10 mV/\(^\circ\)C and no offset. Our job: measure both, with their uncertainties, and check the datasheet. Calibration is an everyday task in instrumentation, and it is a linear regression.

    Least squares: what is the “best” line?#

    For each data point, the residual is measured minus predicted:

    \[ e_i = y_i - \hat y_i . \]

    It is the vertical distance from the point to the line: positive if the point lies above the line, negative if below. Square each residual and add them up to get the residual sum of squares

    \[ \mathrm{RSS} = e_1^2 + e_2^2 + \dots + e_n^2 = \sum_i \bigl(y_i - \hat\beta_0 - \hat\beta_1 x_i\bigr)^2 . \]

    Squaring stops positive and negative residuals from cancelling (without it, a line far above some points and far below others could have residuals adding to zero), and it makes large residuals count heavily. The RSS is in the units of \(y\) squared, here mV\(^2\).

    Least squares chooses, out of all possible straight lines, the one with the smallest RSS.

    The least-squares line#

    The RSS is a function of the two unknowns \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\). At its minimum both partial derivatives are zero. Solving those two equations gives

    \[ \hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x . \]

    In words: the slope is how \(x\) and \(y\) vary together, divided by how much \(x\) varies. The numerator is large and positive when points with \(x\) above average also have \(y\) above average; the denominator measures the spread of \(x\). The second formula says that the least-squares line always passes through the point of averages \((\bar x, \bar y)\).

    Derivation

    Write \(\mathrm{RSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1 x_i)^2\) and use the chain rule.

    \(\partial\mathrm{RSS}/\partial\beta_0 = -2\sum_i (y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1x_i) = 0\) gives \(n\beta_0 = \sum_i y_i - \beta_1\sum_i x_i\), so \(\beta_0 = \bar y - \beta_1\bar x\).

    \(\partial\mathrm{RSS}/\partial\beta_1 = -2\sum_i x_i(y_i - \beta_0 - \beta_1x_i) = 0\). Substitute \(\beta_0\): \(\sum_i x_i\,[(y_i - \bar y) - \beta_1(x_i - \bar x)] = 0\). Since \(\sum_i \bar x (y_i - \bar y) = 0\) and \(\sum_i \bar x (x_i - \bar x) = 0\) (deviations from the average add to zero), \(x_i\) can be replaced by \(x_i - \bar x\), giving \(\beta_1 = \sum_i(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) / \sum_i(x_i - \bar x)^2\).

    Try it yourself before opening this box: it needs only the chain rule.

    Calibration: the result#

    For the 20 readings, the averages are \(\bar T = 47.7\) \(^\circ\)C and \(\bar V = 471.6\) mV, and the least-squares line is

    \[ \hat V = 2.5 + 9.83\,T . \]

    Worked numbers

    From the data: \(\sum_i(T_i - \bar T)(V_i - \bar V) = 154\,992.3\) and \(\sum_i(T_i - \bar T)^2 = 15\,764.0\).

    \(\hat\beta_1 = 154\,992.3 / 15\,764.0 = 9.832\) mV/\(^\circ\)C.

    \(\hat\beta_0 = 471.64 - 9.832 \times 47.71 = 471.64 - 469.09 = 2.55 \approx 2.5\) mV (2.54 with unrounded averages).

    • Sensitivity: \(\hat\beta_1 = 9.83\) mV/\(^\circ\)C, close to the datasheet's 10. Is 9.83 consistent with 10?
    • Offset: \(\hat\beta_0 = 2.5\) mV, where the datasheet says 0. Is 2.5 mV a real offset?

    Every estimate from noisy data differs a little from the truth, so neither question can be answered by looking at the numbers alone. The next three sections answer them.

    Using the calibration. Suppose the sensor reads \(V = 500\) mV. Invert the line:

    \[ T = \frac{V - \hat\beta_0}{\hat\beta_1} = \frac{500 - 2.54}{9.832} \approx 50.6\ ^\circ\mathrm C . \]

    With the datasheet formula (\(T = V/10\)) the answer would be 50.0 \(^\circ\)C; the calibration corrects it by 0.6 \(^\circ\)C. This is the purpose of a calibration: to turn a raw sensor reading into a reliable temperature.

    Example: calibrating a pressure sensor#

    Five readings give the sensor voltage \(V\) (mV) at reference pressures \(P\) (kPa):

    \(P\)0255075100
    \(V\)2.151.8101.0150.6199.5

    Find the least-squares line \(\hat V = \hat\beta_0 + \hat\beta_1 P\), then the pressure for a reading of \(V = 120\) mV.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Averages: \(\bar P = 50\) kPa, \(\bar V = 101.0\) mV.

    \[ \sum_i (P_i - \bar P)(V_i - \bar V) = 12340, \qquad \sum_i (P_i - \bar P)^2 = 6250 \]

    so \(\hat\beta_1 = 12340/6250 = 1.9744\) mV/kPa and \(\hat\beta_0 = \bar V - \hat\beta_1\bar P = 101.0 - 1.9744 \times 50 = 2.28\) mV.

    For \(V = 120\) mV: \(P = (120 - 2.28)/1.9744 = 59.6\) kPa.

    How sure are we? The standard error#

    If we repeated the calibration with 20 new readings, the noise would be different, and so would the fitted slope. The standard error (SE) of the slope is the standard deviation of \(\hat\beta_1\) over many such repetitions. It is the “\(\pm\)” of the result. For simple linear regression

    \[ \mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}} , \]

    the noise divided by the spread of \(x\). Here \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of the noise \(\varepsilon\). The slope is uncertain when the noise is large, and well determined when the \(x\) values are spread out. In practice \(\sigma\) is unknown and is replaced by its estimate from the data, the residual standard error (RSE), defined below.

    A 95% confidence interval for \(\beta_1\) is

    \[ \hat\beta_1 \pm 2\,\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1). \]

    An interval built this way contains the true value in about 95 of every 100 repetitions of the experiment. (The exact multiplier is 2.10 for 20 readings; 2 is the usual rounded value.) Note what “95%” means: it is a property of the method, over many repeats, not a probability attached to this one interval.

    Calibration data. \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 0.13\) mV/\(^\circ\)C, so \(\beta_1 = 9.83 \pm 0.13\) mV/\(^\circ\)C and the 95% interval is \([9.58,\ 10.09]\). The datasheet value, 10, lies inside: 9.83 is only \(0.17/0.13 = 1.3\) standard errors below 10. The measured sensitivity is consistent with the datasheet.

    Worked numbers

    \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = \mathrm{RSE}/\sqrt{15\,764.0} = 15.92/125.55 = 0.127\) mV/\(^\circ\)C.

    Interval: \(9.832 - 2 \times 0.127 = 9.58\); \(9.832 + 2 \times 0.127 = 10.09\).

    Three ways to make the SE smaller, and so the calibration more precise: less noise (a quieter sensor, or averaging), more data, and a wider range of \(x\) (readings spread over the whole working range).

    The standard error of the intercept

    \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)^2 = \sigma^2\,\bigl[1/n + \bar x^2/\sum_i(x_i - \bar x)^2\bigr]\). Here \(15.92^2 \times (1/20 + 47.71^2/15\,764.0) = 253.4 \times (0.05 + 0.1444) = 49.3\), so \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0) = 7.0\) mV. The intercept is uncertain because all the data are far from \(T = 0\).

    Is the effect real? The \(t\)-statistic#

    To test the idea “no effect”, i.e. that the true coefficient is exactly 0 (statistics books call this the null hypothesis), compute

    \[ t = \frac{\text{estimate}}{\text{standard error}} = \frac{\hat\beta_1}{\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1)} . \]

    \(t\) counts how many standard errors the estimate lies from zero. Write \(|t|\) for its size, ignoring the sign; a negative \(t\) is just as strong evidence as a positive one.

    • \(|t|\) large (more than about 2): an estimate this far from zero would be rare if the true value were zero, so the effect is real. Equivalently, the 95% interval does not contain 0.
    • \(|t|\) small: the estimate could be zero plus noise.

    Calibration data. Sensitivity: \(t = 9.832/0.127 = 77.5\). Offset: \(t = 2.54/7.02 = 0.36\). The sensitivity is 77 standard errors from zero; the offset is less than one. These are the two possible outcomes: one clearly real, one indistinguishable from zero.

    Example: the t-statistic of the sensitivity#

    The calibration gives the sensitivity \(\hat\beta_1 = 9.83\) mV/°C with standard error \(\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_1) = 0.12\) mV/°C. (a) Test \(H_0: \beta_1 = 0\) (the null hypothesis that the sensor does not respond). (b) Is the datasheet value of 10 mV/°C consistent with the data?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    (a) \(t = \hat\beta_1/\mathrm{SE} = 9.83/0.12 = 81.9\). This is enormous (anything beyond about 2 is significant at 5%): the sensor certainly responds to temperature.

    (b) Test against 10 instead of 0: \(t = (9.83 - 10)/0.12 = -1.42\). \(|t| < 2\), so the difference from the datasheet is within the noise. A 95% interval is \(9.83 \pm 2 \times 0.12 = [9.59, 10.07]\), which contains 10.

    The \(p\)-value#

    The \(p\)-value answers: if there were no effect, how likely is it that noise alone would give a \(|t|\) at least this large? It is computed from the \(t\) distribution with \(n - 2\) degrees of freedom, and statistical software reports it alongside every coefficient.

    • Small \(p\): noise alone rarely does this, so the effect is probably real. By convention, \(p < 0.05\) is called significant. This is a convention, not a law of nature.
    • Careful: \(p\) is not the probability that the effect is zero. A large \(p\) means “no evidence of an effect”, not “no effect”. With only three readings almost nothing is significant, however real.

    The calibration results in the form software prints them:

    estimateSE\(t\)\(p\)
    offset \(\hat\beta_0\) (mV)2.57.00.360.72
    sensitivity \(\hat\beta_1\) (mV/\(^\circ\)C)9.830.1377.5\(\approx 0\)

    The sensitivity is certainly real (of course: the sensor responds to temperature). The offset is only 0.36 standard errors from zero: an offset of 2.5 mV is exactly what noise alone would often produce, so the data are consistent with the datasheet's zero offset. Conclusion: the sensor meets its datasheet within the uncertainty.

    How good is the fit? RSE and \(R^2\)#

    Two numbers summarise how well the line fits.

    The residual standard error is the typical size of a residual:

    \[ \mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n - 2}} . \]

    It estimates the noise \(\sigma\), in the units of \(y\). We divide by \(n - 2\) rather than \(n\) because two coefficients were estimated from the same data, which makes the residuals slightly smaller than the true errors.

    The \(R^2\) statistic is the fraction of the variation in \(Y\) that the model explains:

    \[ R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}, \qquad \mathrm{TSS} = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2 . \]

    The total sum of squares TSS is the variation of \(y\) around its average before using \(x\); RSS is what is left after the fit; their ratio is the fraction left unexplained. \(R^2 = 0\) means the model explains nothing, \(R^2 = 1\) a perfect fit. \(R^2\) has no units, which makes it easy to compare between problems.

    Calibration data. \(\mathrm{RSE} = 15.9\) mV, close to the true noise of 15 mV. Divided by the sensitivity, \(15.9/9.83 = 1.6\) \(^\circ\)C: a single reading of this sensor is uncertain by about \(\pm 1.6\) \(^\circ\)C. \(R^2 = 0.997\): temperature explains 99.7% of the variation in the output.

    Worked numbers

    \(\mathrm{RSS} = \mathrm{RSE}^2 \times (n - 2) = 15.92^2 \times 18 = 4562\) mV\(^2\). \(\mathrm{TSS} = 1\,528\,457\) mV\(^2\), so \(R^2 = 1 - 4562/1\,528\,457 = 1 - 0.0030 = 0.997\).

    What counts as a “good” \(R^2\) depends on the field. A calibration expects more than 0.99; \(R^2 = 0.9\) would mean a faulty sensor. A model of household electricity use from survey data with \(R^2 = 0.5\) may be excellent, because people are unpredictable.

    Example: RSE and R² from the sums#

    A fit to \(n = 20\) calibration readings has \(\mathrm{RSS} = 4480\) mV² and total sum of squares \(\mathrm{TSS} = \sum(y_i - \bar y)^2 = 1\,515\,000\) mV². Compute the residual standard error and \(R^2\).

    Solution triple-click to reveal
    \[ \mathrm{RSE} = \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-2}} = \sqrt{\frac{4480}{18}} = 15.8\ \mathrm{mV}, \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}} = 1 - \frac{4480}{1\,515\,000} = 0.9970. \]

    A typical reading lies about 15.8 mV from the line, and the line explains 99.70% of the variation in the voltage.
