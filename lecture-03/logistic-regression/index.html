    Logistic regression#

    Classification problems#

    From your survey: is a machine healthy or faulty? Is a milk sample pure or adulterated? Is a patient at high risk of asthma? Is a sensor reading normal or an anomaly? Which fault: imbalance, misalignment or bearing wear?

    All five come from the problems the students named in the survey. The first four have two classes; the last has three.

    Two steps: 1. Estimate the probability of each class. 2. Turn the probability into a decision

    Keeping these two steps apart is the key idea of the lecture. The model gives probabilities (slides 8–15); the decision needs a threshold, which depends on the costs of mistakes (slides 16 and 23).

    The last example has three classes (end of today)

    Slide 32 deals with it.

    Example 1: vibration and motor faults#

    A motor with a worn bearing or loose mounting vibrates more

    Vibration monitoring is the standard tool of predictive maintenance: a sensor (an accelerometer) on the motor housing measures how strongly it shakes. Severity is usually reported as the RMS (root-mean-square) vibration velocity in mm/s, the quantity used in the ISO vibration-severity standards.

    Low vibration: almost always healthy. High vibration: almost always faulty

    Between the two there is a grey zone where both happen.

    Data: 200 motors. \(X\) = vibration \(v\) (RMS velocity, mm/s); \(Y\) = 1 if faulty, 0 if healthy

    114 of the 200 motors are faulty.

    Plot: each mark is one motor: top row faulty, bottom row healthy

    Below about 3 mm/s all motors are healthy; above about 5 mm/s nearly all are faulty; in between, both.

    Symbols

    \(v\)RMS vibration velocity (mm/s).
    RMSRoot mean square: \(\sqrt{\text{mean of the squared signal}}\), a measure of the size of an oscillating signal.

    Why not use linear regression?#

    Fit a straight line to the 0s and 1s: \(\hat y = -0.15 + 0.144\,v\)

    We could treat 0 and 1 as numbers and fit a least-squares line (Lecture 2).

    At 0 mm/s it predicts \(-0.15\). At 10 mm/s it predicts 1.29. A probability must stay between 0 and 1

    \(-0.15 + 0.144 \times 0 = -0.15\) and \(-0.15 + 0.144 \times 10 = 1.29\). Neither is a possible probability.

    We need a curve that stays between 0 and 1

    Also, a straight line rises at the same rate everywhere, while the real probability is flat near 0, rises steeply in the grey zone, and is flat again near 1.

    Plot: red, the straight-line fit; shaded, impossible values

    Worked numbers

    Notebook: intercept \(-0.146\), slope \(0.1438\). At \(v = 10\): \(-0.146 + 1.438 = 1.29\).

    The logistic (sigmoid) function#

    \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\)

    The logistic function, also called the sigmoid (“S-shaped”).

    Any number \(z\) goes in. The output is always between 0 and 1

    \(e^{-z}\) is always positive, so the denominator is always above 1 and the fraction is between 0 and 1.

    \(z = 0\) gives \(\sigma = 0.5\)

    \(1/(1 + e^0) = 1/2\).

    Large positive \(z\): close to 1. Large negative \(z\): close to 0

    \(\sigma(4) = 1/(1 + e^{-4}) = 1/1.018 = 0.982\); \(\sigma(-4) = 1/(1 + 54.6) = 0.018\).

    The same Greek letter now names a function. Point this out explicitly: it is a common source of confusion.

    Plot: an S-shaped curve (“sigmoid” means S-shaped)

    Symbols

    \(\sigma(z)\)Logistic (sigmoid) function.
    \(e\)Euler's number, 2.718…

    The logistic regression model#

    Put the straight line inside the sigmoid: \(p(X) = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta_1X) = 1/(1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1X)})\)

    The linear part is the same as in Lecture 2; the sigmoid squeezes it into \((0, 1)\).

    The line can be any number. The sigmoid turns it into a probability. \(\beta_1 > 0\): \(p\) rises with \(X\)

    If \(\beta_1 < 0\), \(p\) falls with \(X\).

    \(\beta_1\) large: a sharp step. \(\beta_1\) small: a gentle slope

    The steepness of the S is set by \(\beta_1\); its position by where the line crosses zero, \(X = -\beta_0/\beta_1\).

    Plot: blue (\(\beta_1 = 2.5\)) crosses 0.5 at \(X = 4\); green (\(\beta_1 = 0.8\)) at \(X = 5\). \(p = 0.5\) where the line is zero: \(X = -\beta_0/\beta_1\)

    Blue: \(\beta_0 = -10\), so \(X = 10/2.5 = 4\). Green: \(\beta_0 = -4\), so \(X = 4/0.8 = 5\).

    Symbols

    \(\beta_0\), \(\beta_1\)Intercept and slope of the line inside the sigmoid.

    Odds and log-odds#

    Odds = probability of yes / probability of no: odds \(= p/(1 - p)\). \(p = 0.5\): odds 1. \(p = 0.8\): odds 4

    Odds are common in everyday language (“four to one”). \(0.8/0.2 = 4\).

    For logistic regression, the log-odds is a straight line: \(\ln[p/(1 - p)] = \beta_0 + \beta_1X\)

    Solving \(p = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\) for \(z\) gives \(z = \ln[p/(1 - p)]\). So logistic regression is linear regression for the log-odds.

    Reading \(\beta_1\): increase \(X\) by 1: the log-odds increase by \(\beta_1\). So the odds are multiplied by \(e^{\beta_1}\)

    Adding \(\beta_1\) to a logarithm multiplies the quantity by \(e^{\beta_1}\). Note: it is the odds, not the probability, that change by a fixed factor.

    Symbols

    odds\(p/(1 - p)\), between 0 and \(\infty\).
    \(\ln\)Natural logarithm.
    Beyond the slide: from \(p\) to the log-odds

    \(p = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\) gives \(1 - p = e^{-z}/(1 + e^{-z})\), so \(p/(1 - p) = e^{z}\) and \(\ln[p/(1 - p)] = z\).

    Example: probability, odds and log-odds#

    A fitted model for motor faults is \(\log\frac{p}{1-p} = -6 + 1.5\,x\), with \(x\) the vibration level (mm/s). (a) Find the probability of a fault at \(x = 5\). (b) At which vibration is the probability 50%? (c) By what factor do the odds grow for each extra 1 mm/s?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    (a) \(z = -6 + 1.5 \times 5 = 1.5\), so \(p = 1/(1 + e^{-1.5}) = 0.818\). The odds are \(e^{1.5} = 4.48\).

    (b) \(p = 0.5\) when \(z = 0\): \(x = 6/1.5 = 4\) mm/s.

    (c) The log-odds rise by 1.5 per mm/s, so the odds are multiplied by \(e^{1.5} = 4.48\).

    What is a likelihood?#

    \(q\)0.50.70.9
    \(L(q)\)0.00100.00220.0005

    Students will need the idea of likelihood on the next slide. Introduce it first with a coin, where the answer is obvious, so the idea is clear before it is applied to motors.

    Likelihood \(L\): the probability of getting the data we actually observed, if the model's parameters had a given value

    The data are fixed (they have been measured); the parameter is what we vary. For each possible parameter value, the likelihood says how probable our actual data would have been.

    Maximum likelihood: choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable

    The principle behind most fitting methods in the course.

    Example: a coin. 10 tosses give 7 heads and 3 tails. \(L(q) = q^7(1 - q)^3\). \(q = 0.5\): 0.0010; \(q = 0.7\): 0.0022; \(q = 0.9\): 0.0005. Best: \(\hat q = 0.7\) = 7 heads out of 10

    Each head has probability \(q\) and each tail \(1 - q\); the tosses are independent, so the probabilities multiply. The likelihood is largest at \(q = 0.7\), which is the obvious answer, the fraction of heads.

    Worked numbers

    \(0.5^7 \times 0.5^3 = 0.5^{10} = 0.000977\). \(0.7^7 \times 0.3^3 = 0.0824 \times 0.027 = 0.00222\). \(0.9^7 \times 0.1^3 = 0.478 \times 0.001 = 0.00048\).

    Beyond the slide: why \(\hat q = 7/10\)

    \(\ln L = 7\ln q + 3\ln(1 - q)\). Its derivative \(7/q - 3/(1 - q)\) is zero when \(7(1 - q) = 3q\), so \(q = 0.7\).

    How do we fit it? Maximum likelihood#

    For each motor, the model gives a probability of what we saw: faulty (\(y_i = 1\)): probability \(p_i\); healthy (\(y_i = 0\)): probability \(1 - p_i\)

    For given coefficients, the model assigns each motor a probability of being faulty, \(p_i = \sigma(\beta_0 + \beta_1v_i)\). The probability of what actually happened is \(p_i\) if the motor was faulty and \(1 - p_i\) if healthy.

    Multiply over all motors: \(L(\bb) = \prod_{y_i = 1} p_i \times \prod_{y_i = 0}(1 - p_i)\)

    The motors are independent, so the probability of the whole dataset is the product. \(L\) is called the likelihood.

    Maximum likelihood: choose the \(\beta\)'s that make the data we observed most probable, as with the coin

    Good coefficients give high \(p_i\) to faulty motors and low \(p_i\) to healthy ones, so \(L\) is large. The coin had one parameter \(q\); the motors have two, \(\beta_0\) and \(\beta_1\), and each motor has its own probability \(p_i\).

    Linear regression minimised the RSS; least squares is also maximum likelihood when the noise is Gaussian (ESL 2.6.3).

    Symbols

    \(p_i\)Model's probability that motor \(i\) is faulty.
    \(\prod\)Product (multiply all terms).
    \(L(\bb)\)Likelihood of the coefficients \(\bb\).

    Loss and cost for classification: cross-entropy#

    Loss for one motor: \(\ell_i = -\ln p_i\) if faulty (\(y_i = 1\)); \(-\ln(1 - p_i)\) if healthy (\(y_i = 0\))

    The same loss/cost language as Lecture 2. The loss of one motor is minus the log of the probability the model gave to what really happened.

    Cost = average loss \(= -\frac1n\ln L\): \(J(\bb) = -\frac1n\sum_i[y_i\ln p_i + (1 - y_i)\ln(1 - p_i)]\)

    The logarithm turns the product \(L\) into a sum (easier to compute, and it avoids numbers too small for the computer). The minus sign turns maximising into minimising, and \(\frac1n\) makes it an average.

    Since \(y_i\) is 0 or 1, only one of the two terms is non-zero for each motor

    When \(y_i = 1\) the second term has the factor \(1 - y_i = 0\), and vice versa. The one formula covers both cases of the loss.

    Loss of one faulty motor: \(p_i = 0.99\) (confident, right): 0.01; \(p_i = 0.5\) (unsure): \(\ln 2 = 0.69\); \(p_i = 0.01\) (confident, wrong): 4.6

    \(-\ln 0.99 = 0.01\), \(-\ln 0.5 = 0.69\), \(-\ln 0.01 = 4.6\). Confident mistakes are punished heavily.

    Minimising the cost = maximising the likelihood. The cost is called the cross-entropy or log loss

    The name comes from information theory. The same cost trains neural-network classifiers (Lecture 7).

    Symbols

    \(\ell_i\)Loss of motor \(i\).
    \(J(\bb)\)Cost: average cross-entropy.

    Example: the cost of a confident mistake#

    For one machine the model gives \(p = 0.9\) for “faulty”. Compute the cross-entropy loss \(-[y\ln p + (1-y)\ln(1-p)]\) if the machine really is faulty (\(y = 1\)) and if it is healthy (\(y = 0\)).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    \(y = 1\): loss \(= -\ln 0.9 = 0.105\). Small: the model was confident and right.

    \(y = 0\): loss \(= -\ln(1 - 0.9) = -\ln 0.1 = 2.303\). About 22 times larger: a confident wrong answer is punished hard.

    Gradient descent for logistic regression#

    There is no formula for the best \(\bb\). We walk downhill: \(\bb \leftarrow \bb + \frac{\alpha}{n}\bX\T(\by - \bp)\)

    Setting the derivatives of \(J\) to zero gives equations that cannot be solved by algebra, so we use gradient descent (Lecture 2). The gradient of the cross-entropy is \(-\frac1n\bX\T(\by - \bp)\).

    Compare with linear regression (Lecture 2): \(\bb \leftarrow \bb + \frac{\alpha}{n}\bX\T(\by - \bX\bb)\)

    The same rule! Step \(\propto\) (measured \(-\) predicted) \(\times\) input. Only the prediction changes: a probability instead of a straight line

    This is not a coincidence; both come from maximum likelihood.

    Motors: the cost falls from 0.693 to 0.140. LogisticRegression in scikit-learn gives the same answer

    At the start all coefficients are zero, so every \(p_i = 0.5\) and the cost is \(\ln 2 = 0.693\). After 3000 steps (standardised input, \(\alpha = 1\)) it is 0.140. scikit-learn uses a faster method (Newton-type), with the same result.

    Symbols

    \(\bp\)Column of predicted probabilities.
    \(\alpha\)Step size.
    Beyond the slide: no regularisation in scikit-learn

    By default scikit-learn's LogisticRegression adds a penalty on large coefficients (regularisation, Lecture 4). The notebook uses C=np.inf to switch it off, so that the result is pure maximum likelihood, as in ISLP.

    Motors: the result#

    Plot: blue, fitted curve; red, fraction faulty in each 1-mm/s bin

    The red dots are simple counts: for example, of the motors between 4 and 5 mm/s, 62% were faulty. The fitted S-curve passes through them.

    \(\hat p(v) = \sigma(-10.1 + 2.39\,v)\)

    The maximum-likelihood fit.

    50% point: \(-10.1 + 2.39\,v = 0\), so \(v = 4.2\) mm/s

    Where the line inside the sigmoid is zero. The true value in the simulation is 4.0 mm/s.

    Each extra mm/s multiplies the odds of a fault by \(e^{2.39} = 10.9\)

    Slide 10 in action.

    \(\hat\beta_1 = 2.39\), SE \(= 0.45\), \(z = 2.39/0.45 = 5.3\): a real effect

    As in Lecture 2, each coefficient has a standard error. The ratio is called \(z\) here (ISLP Table 4.1); \(\vert z\vert > 2\) means the effect is real.

    In maintenance words: below 2 mm/s, under 1% fault chance. Above 6 mm/s, over 98%

    Symbols

    \(\hat p(v)\)Estimated probability of a fault at vibration \(v\).
    \(z\)Estimate divided by its standard error.

    Worked numbers

    50% point: \(10.10/2.386 = 4.24\) mm/s. Odds factor: \(e^{2.386} = 10.9\). \(z = 2.386/0.452 = 5.3\).

    \(\hat p(2) = \sigma(-10.10 + 4.77) = \sigma(-5.33) = 0.005\); \(\hat p(6) = \sigma(-10.10 + 14.32) = \sigma(4.22) = 0.985\).

    Beyond the slide: where the SE comes from

    \(\mathrm{SE}^2\) is the diagonal of \((\bX\T\mathbf W\bX)^{-1}\), with \(\mathbf W\) diagonal, \(W_{ii} = p_i(1 - p_i)\) (ESL 4.4.1). The notebook computes this.

    From a probability to a decision#

    To classify, choose a threshold, usually 0.5: predict 1 if \(\hat p \ge 0.5\)

    With threshold 0.5 we predict the more probable class.

    \(\hat p \ge 0.5\) is the same as \(\beta_0 + \beta_1X \ge 0\)

    Because \(\sigma(z) \ge 0.5\) exactly when \(z \ge 0\).

    The decision boundary is where \(\beta_0 + \beta_1X = 0\). For the motors: \(v = 4.2\) mm/s

    Motors above 4.2 mm/s are called faulty.

    Logistic regression gives a probability. The threshold turns it into a yes/no decision. We choose the threshold (later today)

    How to choose it is slide 23.

    Example 2: two sensors on each machine#

    A monitoring system records two inputs for each machine: vibration \(v\) (mm/s); bearing temperature \(T\) (\(^\circ\)C)

    A failing bearing both vibrates and heats up through friction. Using both sensors should classify better than either alone.

    Faulty machines vibrate more and run hotter, but the groups overlap

    The overlap means some errors are unavoidable (the Bayes error of Lecture 1).

    Data: 400 training machines (122 faulty), 2000 test machines (593 faulty)

    A separate test set, as Lecture 1 taught: every performance number is measured on the 2000 machines not used in training.

    Plot: training machines (simulated)

    Healthy machines form a compact cloud; faulty ones are more spread out and stretched along a diagonal, because in faulty machines high vibration and high temperature go together.

    Two inputs: the decision boundary is a line#

    \(\hat p = \sigma(-15.8 + 1.28\,v + 0.199\,T)\)

    Logistic regression with two inputs.

    The boundary \(\hat p = 0.5\) is where \(-15.8 + 1.28\,v + 0.199\,T = 0\)

    This is a straight line: \(T = 79.6 - 6.41\,v\)

    Solve for \(T\). A machine at 2 mm/s is called faulty above \(79.6 - 12.8 = 66.8~^\circ\)C; at 5 mm/s, above \(47.5~^\circ\)C. High vibration and high temperature can each push a machine over the line.

    Above the line: faulty. Below the line: healthy

    Worked numbers

    \(15.84/0.1991 = 79.6\); \(1.276/0.1991 = 6.41\).
