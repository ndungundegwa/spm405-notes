    Regularisation#

    Too many inputs: an electronic nose#

    A gas-sensor array (“electronic nose”) smells a milk sample with 40 channels

    Each channel is a metal-oxide or polymer sensor that responds to a range of volatile compounds. Adulterants change the smell pattern. Milk adulteration was a problem named in the survey.

    Goal: predict the adulterant concentration (%). Data: only 50 samples. Only 5 channels really matter

    In a real array we would not know which channels matter; finding them is part of the task.

    Least squares, 41 coefficients: training MSE 0.22; test MSE 4.61. Guessing the average: 8.30

    41 coefficients (40 channels plus intercept) from 50 samples. The training error (0.22) is far below the noise variance (1.0): a sure sign of overfitting (Lecture 1). On new samples the error is 4.61.

    Almost as many coefficients as samples: least squares fits the noise

    With \(p \ge n\) least squares has no unique answer at all.

    Symbols

    \(p\)Number of inputs (40 channels).

    Choosing inputs: subset selection#

    Best subset: try every combination of inputs, keep the best by CV. \(2^{40} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12}\) models. Far too many

    Each channel is either in or out: \(2^{40} = 1\,099\,511\,627\,776\) combinations.

    Forward stepwise: start with no inputs; add the one that helps most; repeat. About 800 fits

    Step 1 tries 40 models, step 2 tries 39, and so on: \(40 + 39 + \dots + 1 = 820\). Fast, but it can miss the best combination (ISLP 6.1.2).

    A better idea: keep all the inputs, but shrink their coefficients towards zero. This is regularisation. Shrink: make smaller in size, towards 0

    Ridge regression#

    Minimise \(\mathrm{RSS} + \lambda\sum_j\beta_j^2\): fit the data + keep coefficients small

    The first term rewards fitting the data; the second penalises large coefficients. \(\lambda\) sets the balance.

    \(\lambda = 0\): least squares. \(\lambda\) large: all \(\beta_j\) close to 0. The intercept \(\beta_0\) is not shrunk

    Between the two extremes lies a range of models from flexible to rigid, controlled by one number.

    The sum starts at \(j = 1\): shrinking the intercept would only shift all predictions towards zero, which is never wanted.

    Standardise first: the penalty treats all coefficients alike, so all inputs must be on the same scale

    A coefficient's size depends on the units of its input (per volt or per millivolt). Standardising makes the penalty fair.

    Why it helps: a little bias buys a large drop in variance (Lecture 1)

    Shrunk coefficients change less from one training set to another.

    Symbols

    \(\lambda\)Tuning parameter (\(\ge 0\)); scikit-learn calls it alpha.
    \(\beta_j^2\)Square of coefficient \(j\).
    Beyond the slide: the ridge formula

    \(\hat\bb_{\text{ridge}} = (\bX\T\bX + \lambda\mathbf I)^{-1}\bX\T\by\) (ESL 3.4.1). Adding \(\lambda\) to the diagonal makes the matrix invertible even when \(p > n\).

    Ridge: the coefficient path#

    Plot: blue, the 5 channels that matter; grey, 3 of the 35 that do not

    Each line is one coefficient as \(\lambda\) increases from 0.01 to 10 000.

    As \(\lambda\) grows, every coefficient shrinks smoothly towards 0. None becomes exactly 0: ridge keeps all 40 channels

    A log scale, because the interesting range of \(\lambda\) covers many powers of ten.

    Example: ridge shrinkage with one input#

    With one standardised input and no intercept, least squares gives \(\hat\beta = \sum x_iy_i / \sum x_i^2\) and ridge gives \(\hat\beta_\lambda = \sum x_iy_i / (\sum x_i^2 + \lambda)\). If \(\sum x_iy_i = 24\) and \(\sum x_i^2 = 8\), compute \(\hat\beta_\lambda\) for \(\lambda = 0, 2, 8, 24\).

    The coefficient shrinks smoothly towards 0 but never reaches it: ridge keeps every input.

    The lasso#

    Minimise \(\mathrm{RSS} + \lambda\sum_j\vert \beta_j\vert \): absolute values

    The only change from ridge: absolute values instead of squares.

    Also shrinks the coefficients. But sets some exactly to zero. So it also selects inputs

    Slide 19 explains why.

    Name: least absolute shrinkage and selection operator

    Introduced by Robert Tibshirani (one of the ISLP and ESL authors) in 1996.

    Result: a simpler model you can interpret: which channels matter?

    Symbols

    \(\vert \beta_j\vert \)Absolute value of coefficient \(j\).

    Lasso: the coefficient path#

    Coefficients hit exactly zero one after another. The useless channels (grey) go first. At large \(\lambda\) nothing is left

    Reading from right to left, the path shows the order in which channels enter the model as \(\lambda\) decreases: the important ones first.

    Why does the lasso give zeros?#

    Axes: two coefficients, \(\beta_1\) and \(\beta_2\). Red ellipses: contours of equal RSS around the least-squares answer

    Each ellipse is a set of \((\beta_1, \beta_2)\) with the same RSS, like a contour line on a map; the RSS grows away from the least-squares point.

    Green: the allowed region. Lasso: a diamond with corners on the axes. Ridge: a circle

    The penalised problem is equivalent to minimising the RSS subject to \(\vert \beta_1\vert + \vert \beta_2\vert \le s\) (a diamond) or \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le s\) (a circle).

    The ellipse usually first touches the diamond at a corner, where a coefficient is zero

    The solution is where the smallest ellipse meets the region. A diamond has sharp corners on the axes; a circle has none, so ridge solutions are almost never exactly on an axis (ISLP Figure 6.7).

    The budget \(s\) plays the role of \(\lambda\): a small budget is a strong penalty.

    Example: the lasso sets small coefficients to zero#

    For orthonormal inputs the lasso solution is the least-squares coefficient moved towards zero by \(\lambda/2\), and set to 0 if it would cross zero: \(\hat\beta_j^{\text{lasso}} = \operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\,\max(|\hat\beta_j| - \lambda/2,\ 0)\). With least-squares coefficients \(2.4, -0.3, 0.9, -1.6\) and \(\lambda = 1\), find the lasso coefficients. Which inputs are dropped?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Move each towards 0 by 0.5: \(2.4 \to 1.9\), \(-0.3 \to 0\) (it would cross zero, so it is set to 0), \(0.9 \to 0.4\), \(-1.6 \to -1.1\).

    Input 2 is dropped: the lasso does variable selection, ridge does not.

    Choosing \(\lambda\): cross-validation#

    1. Choose a grid of \(\lambda\) values. 2. For each \(\lambda\), compute the 10-fold CV error. 3. Keep the \(\lambda\) with the smallest CV error. 4. Refit on all the training data with that \(\lambda\)

    Part 1 and Part 2 meet: cross-validation chooses the amount of regularisation. The grid in the notebook has 60 values, evenly spaced on a log scale.

    In Python: RidgeCV, LassoCV (scikit-learn) do all four steps

    The ridge grid in the notebook; the lasso grid runs from 0.001 to 10. The name clash with the step size catches many students.

    Table: guess the average 8.30 (0 channels); least squares 4.61 (40); ridge (CV) 3.88 (40); lasso (CV) 1.38 (15). Noise variance 1.0

    All on the same 1000 test samples. The lasso comes close to the best possible error of 1.0. Ridge helps less, because it keeps all 35 useless channels.

    The lasso's choice: it keeps all 5 true channels, with the 5 largest coefficients: channel 3: true 1.5, lasso 1.41; 11: \(-1.0\), \(-0.81\); 19: 0.8, 0.54; 27: 1.2, 1.49; 35: \(-0.7\), \(-0.52\)

    The signs are all right. The sizes are shrunk towards zero (except channel 27), as the penalty intends.

    The other 10 kept are small (at most 0.22)

    A dairy engineer could drop them with little loss. CV tends to keep a few extra inputs, because a slightly smaller \(\lambda\) costs little.

    Worked numbers

    Improvement over least squares: \(4.61/1.38 = 3.3\) times smaller test error. Ridge's \(\lambda\) chosen by CV was 60; on the test data the best ridge \(\lambda\) would have been about 4 (test MSE 2.57). CV estimates from 50 samples are noisy (slide 23).

    Ridge or lasso?#

    RidgeLasso
    Many inputs each matter a littleA few inputs matter, the rest do not
    Keeps every inputDrops inputs: easier to interpret
    Handles correlated inputs smoothlyPicks one of a correlated group

    Ridge: many inputs each matter a little; keeps every input; handles correlated inputs smoothly

    Lasso: a few inputs matter, the rest do not; drops inputs: easier to interpret; picks one of a correlated group

    When two inputs are nearly identical, ridge shares the coefficient between them; the lasso tends to keep one and drop the other, somewhat arbitrarily. The elastic net mixes the two penalties.

    You rarely know in advance. Try both and compare by cross-validation

    Things to watch#

    Standardise inputs before ridge and lasso, using the training data only

    Every step inside the folds: scaling, choosing inputs, choosing \(\lambda\)

    CV estimates vary: with small data, two \(\lambda\) values with similar CV error are equally good; prefer the simpler model

    The “one-standard-error rule” (ISLP 6.2.3): choose the simplest model whose CV error is within one SE of the minimum.

    Final check: if you have a separate test set, touch it only once, at the very end

    A test set used to make choices is no longer a test set.

    Summary#

    1. Cross-validation estimates the test error from one dataset; use it to choose models
    1. \(k\)-fold CV with \(k = 5\) or 10 is the usual choice
    1. The bootstrap gives the uncertainty of almost any estimate
    1. Ridge and lasso shrink coefficients to control variance; the lasso also selects inputs
    1. Choose \(\lambda\) by cross-validation, with inputs standardised
