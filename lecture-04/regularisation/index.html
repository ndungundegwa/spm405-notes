    Regularisation#

    When a model has almost as many inputs as there are observations, least squares fits the noise. This page shows two ways out: choosing a subset of the inputs, and regularisation, which keeps all the inputs but shrinks their coefficients towards zero. The two regularised methods, ridge regression and the lasso, are tuned by the cross-validation of the previous page.

    Too many inputs: an electronic nose#

    An electronic nose is an array of gas sensors. Each channel is a metal-oxide or polymer sensor that responds to a range of volatile compounds, each a little differently, so a sample gives a pattern of readings. An adulterant (a substance added illegally to milk, such as water or urea) changes that pattern.

    The task: predict the adulterant concentration (%) of a milk sample from its 40 channel readings. We have only \(n = 50\) samples. In the simulated data only 5 channels really matter; in a real array we would not know which, and finding them is part of the task.

    Least squares fits 41 coefficients (40 channels plus the intercept) from 50 samples:

    MSE
    least squares, training data0.22
    least squares, new test samples4.61
    guessing the average concentration8.30
    noise variance (the best possible)1.0

    The training error, 0.22, is far below the noise variance, 1.0: a sure sign of overfitting. With almost as many coefficients as samples, least squares fits the noise. (With \(p \ge n\) it has no unique answer at all.)

    Choosing inputs: subset selection#

    One remedy is to use only some of the inputs.

    • Best subset selection tries every combination of inputs and keeps the best by cross-validation. But each of the 40 channels is either in or out, giving \(2^{40} = 1\,099\,511\,627\,776 \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12}\) models. Far too many.
    • Forward stepwise selection starts with no inputs, adds the one that helps most, and repeats. Step 1 tries 40 models, step 2 tries 39, and so on: \(40 + 39 + \dots + 1 = 820\) fits. It is fast, but it can miss the best combination (ISLP Section 6.1.2).

    A better idea: keep all the inputs, but shrink their coefficients towards zero (make them smaller in size). This is regularisation.

    Ridge regression#

    Ridge regression chooses the coefficients that minimise

    \[ \underbrace{\mathrm{RSS}}_{\text{fit the data}} \; + \; \underbrace{\lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \beta_j^2}_{\text{keep coefficients small}} . \]

    The first term rewards fitting the data; the second, the penalty, punishes large coefficients. The tuning parameter \(\lambda\) (Greek “lambda”, \(\lambda \ge 0\)) sets the balance:

    • \(\lambda = 0\): no penalty, ordinary least squares;
    • \(\lambda\) large: all \(\beta_j\) are pushed close to 0.

    Between the two extremes lies a whole range of models, from flexible to rigid, controlled by one number. The sum starts at \(j = 1\): the intercept \(\beta_0\) is not shrunk, because shrinking it would only pull all predictions towards zero.

    Standardise the inputs first. The penalty treats all coefficients alike, but a coefficient's size depends on the units of its input (per volt or per millivolt). Standardising every input to mean 0 and spread 1 (\(z = (x - \bar x)/s\)) makes the penalty fair.

    Why it helps: the bias–variance trade-off of Lecture 1. Shrunk coefficients change less from one training set to another (lower variance); in exchange they are slightly biased towards zero. A little bias buys a large drop in variance.

    The ridge formula

    In matrix form (ESL Section 3.4.1), \(\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}_{\text{ridge}} = (\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X + \lambda I)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y\), where \(I\) is the identity matrix. Adding \(\lambda\) to the diagonal makes the matrix invertible even when \(p > n\).

    The coefficient path. Plot each coefficient against \(\lambda\) (on a logarithmic scale, because the interesting range of \(\lambda\) covers many powers of ten):

    As \(\lambda\) grows, every coefficient shrinks smoothly towards 0, but none becomes exactly 0: ridge keeps all 40 channels.

    Example: ridge shrinkage with one input#

    With one standardised input and no intercept, least squares gives \(\hat\beta = \sum x_iy_i / \sum x_i^2\) and ridge gives \(\hat\beta_\lambda = \sum x_iy_i / (\sum x_i^2 + \lambda)\). If \(\sum x_iy_i = 24\) and \(\sum x_i^2 = 8\), compute \(\hat\beta_\lambda\) for \(\lambda = 0, 2, 8, 24\).

    The lasso#

    The lasso (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator, introduced by Robert Tibshirani in 1996) minimises

    \[ \mathrm{RSS} + \lambda \sum_{j=1}^{p} \lvert\beta_j\rvert . \]

    The only change from ridge: absolute values \(\lvert\beta_j\rvert\) (the size of \(\beta_j\) without its sign) instead of squares. Like ridge, the lasso shrinks the coefficients. Unlike ridge, it sets some of them exactly to zero, so it also selects inputs. The result is a simpler model you can interpret: which channels matter?

    Why does the lasso give zeros?#

    The penalised problems are equivalent to minimising the RSS subject to a budget \(s\) on the size of the coefficients. With two coefficients:

    • lasso: \(\lvert\beta_1\rvert + \lvert\beta_2\rvert \le s\), a diamond with corners on the axes;
    • ridge: \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 \le s\), a circle.

    The budget \(s\) plays the role of \(\lambda\): a small budget is a strong penalty.

    The solution is where the smallest RSS ellipse first touches the allowed region. A diamond has sharp corners on the axes, and the ellipse usually touches it at a corner, where one coefficient is exactly zero. A circle has no corners, so ridge solutions are almost never exactly on an axis (ISLP Figure 6.7).

    Example: the lasso sets small coefficients to zero#

    For orthonormal inputs the lasso solution is the least-squares coefficient moved towards zero by \(\lambda/2\), and set to 0 if it would cross zero: \(\hat\beta_j^{\text{lasso}} = \operatorname{sign}(\hat\beta_j)\,\max(|\hat\beta_j| - \lambda/2,\ 0)\). With least-squares coefficients \(2.4, -0.3, 0.9, -1.6\) and \(\lambda = 1\), find the lasso coefficients. Which inputs are dropped?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Move each towards 0 by 0.5: \(2.4 \to 1.9\), \(-0.3 \to 0\) (it would cross zero, so it is set to 0), \(0.9 \to 0.4\), \(-1.6 \to -1.1\).

    Input 2 is dropped: the lasso does variable selection, ridge does not.

    Choosing \(\lambda\) by cross-validation#

    Resampling and regularisation meet here: cross-validation chooses the amount of regularisation.

    1. Choose a grid of \(\lambda\) values to try, e.g. 60 values evenly spaced on a logarithmic scale from 0.001 to 1000.
    2. For each \(\lambda\), compute the 10-fold cross-validation error.
    3. Keep the \(\lambda\) with the smallest CV error.
    4. Refit on all the training data with that \(\lambda\).

    In Python, scikit-learn's RidgeCV and LassoCV do all four steps. (They call the penalty alpha; it is \(\lambda\), not the step size \(\alpha\) of gradient descent.)

    Electronic nose: the result#

    All methods scored on the same 1000 new test samples (noise variance 1.0, the best possible test MSE):

    Methodtest MSEchannels used
    Guess the average8.300
    Least squares4.6140
    Ridge (\(\lambda\) by CV)3.8840
    Lasso (\(\lambda\) by CV)1.3815

    The lasso comes close to the best possible error of 1.0, a test error 3.3 times smaller than least squares. Ridge helps less, because it keeps all 35 useless channels.

    The lasso keeps all 5 true channels, and they have its 5 largest coefficients:

    channel311192735
    true coefficient1.5\(-1.0\)0.81.2\(-0.7\)
    lasso estimate1.41\(-0.81\)0.541.49\(-0.52\)

    The signs are all right, and the sizes are shrunk towards zero (except channel 27), as the penalty intends. The other 10 channels it keeps have small coefficients (at most 0.22); a dairy engineer could drop them with little loss. Cross-validation tends to keep a few extra inputs, because a slightly smaller \(\lambda\) costs little.

    Worked numbers

    Improvement over least squares: \(4.61/1.38 = 3.3\) times smaller test error. Ridge's \(\lambda\) chosen by CV was 60; on the test data the best ridge \(\lambda\) would have been about 4 (test MSE 2.57). CV estimates from only 50 samples are noisy.

    Ridge or lasso?#

    RidgeLasso
    suits problems where many inputs each matter a littlesuits problems where a few inputs matter and the rest do not
    keeps every inputdrops inputs: easier to interpret
    handles correlated inputs smoothly, sharing the coefficient between thempicks one of a correlated group, somewhat arbitrarily

    The elastic net mixes the two penalties. You rarely know in advance which suits a problem: try both and compare them by cross-validation.

    Things to watch#

    • Standardise the inputs before ridge and lasso, using the training data only.
    • Every step inside the folds: scaling, choosing inputs, choosing \(\lambda\).
    • CV estimates vary. With small data, two \(\lambda\) values with similar CV error are equally good; prefer the simpler model. The one-standard-error rule (ISLP Section 6.2.3): choose the simplest model whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum.
    • Touch the test set only once, at the very end. A test set used to make choices is no longer a test set.

    Summary of Lecture 4#

    1. Cross-validation estimates the test error from one data set; use it to choose models and hyperparameters.
    2. \(k\)-fold CV with \(k = 5\) or 10 is the usual choice.
    3. The bootstrap gives the uncertainty of almost any estimate.
    4. Ridge and lasso shrink coefficients to control variance; the lasso also selects inputs.
    5. Choose \(\lambda\) by cross-validation, with the inputs standardised.
