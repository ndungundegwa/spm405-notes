    Beyond straight lines#

    Every model so far was linear in its inputs, plus whatever products and powers we chose to add by hand (in Lecture 2 we had to decide to add \(G \times T\) or to take \(\ln V\)). This page introduces models that find the shape of a curve from the data: step functions, regression splines, smoothing splines, local regression and generalised additive models. The running example is the electricity demand of an office building through the day.

    Example: a building's electricity demand#

    A smart meter records the power drawn by an office building. The demand is low at night, has a morning peak at about 9 am and a larger evening peak at about 7:30 pm. A load profile like this is what an energy manager or a utility wants to predict, for example to size a solar system or to plan tariffs. Our data are 120 readings taken at random times, and the goal is to predict the demand from the hour.

    Throughout this page, models are compared by their 10-fold cross-validation error (Lecture 4), in kW\(^2\). The noise variance, the best possible MSE, is 16 kW\(^2\).

    Polynomials and step functions#

    Polynomials. Add \(x^2, x^3, \dots, x^d\) as extra inputs (Lecture 2). This gives one formula for the whole day. A polynomial is global: changing the fit at 9 am changes it everywhere. A low degree is too stiff: a degree-4 polynomial has too few turning points to follow the night–peak–dip–peak–night shape. A high degree can follow it, but swings wildly near 0 h and 24 h, where data are few.

    Step functions. Cut the day into bins and predict the average demand in each bin. This is simple, but the prediction jumps at the bin edges, and a peak that lies across two bins is smeared out.

    model10-fold CV error (kW\(^2\))
    straight line125.6
    polynomial, degree 4112.2
    step function, 6 bins of 4 h80.7

    All three are far above the noise variance of 16.

    Regression splines#

    A regression spline combines the best of both:

    1. Choose knots: points that cut the range of \(x\) into pieces.
    2. Fit a cubic polynomial (degree 3) in each piece.
    3. Force the pieces to join smoothly: at each knot, neighbouring pieces must have the same value, the same slope and the same curvature (second derivative).

    The joining conditions make the result look like one smooth curve. Each piece only has to follow its own part of the day, so there are no wild swings, and a change in one place stays local. A cubic spline with \(K\) interior knots has \(K + 4\) coefficients (ISLP Section 7.4).

    A spline is still linear in the coefficients: it can be written as a sum of fixed basis functions (B-splines) times coefficients, so it is fitted by least squares exactly as in Lecture 2. In Python, scikit-learn's SplineTransformer turns the hour into B-spline columns and LinearRegression then fits them. (Its n_knots counts all knots, evenly spaced over the data, including the two ends.)

    Natural splines

    A natural spline is also forced to be a straight line beyond the outer knots, which calms the behaviour at the ends of the range (ISLP Section 7.4.4).

    Four fits of the same data#

    The spline with 9 knots has a CV error of 30.3 kW\(^2\), four times smaller than the straight line. Most of the remaining gap (30 against the noise of 16) is the effect of outdoor temperature, which this model ignores; we add it below.

    • Too few knots cannot follow the peaks. With few knots the result also depends on where the evenly spaced knots happen to fall relative to the peaks, which is why 5 knots does worse than 4.
    • From 6 to about 15 knots the pieces are short enough to follow both peaks, and the fits are about equally good.
    • Too many knots start to fit the noise.

    The number of knots is a flexibility setting, a hyperparameter, playing the role the polynomial degree played in Lecture 4, and it is chosen the same way: by cross-validation.

    Two relatives: smoothing splines and local regression#

    Smoothing splines put a knot at every data point and control the wiggliness with a penalty on curvature. The fitted curve \(g\) minimises

    \[ \mathrm{RSS} + \lambda \int g''(t)^2\, dt . \]

    Here \(g''(t)\) is the second derivative (curvature) of \(g\) at \(t\), and the integral \(\int \dots\,dt\) adds up its square over the whole range of \(x\). The idea is the same as ridge regression (Lecture 4): \(\lambda = 0\) lets the curve pass through every point; \(\lambda \to \infty\) forces a straight line (which has zero curvature). \(\lambda\) is chosen by cross-validation (ISLP Section 7.5).

    Local regression (also called LOESS) predicts at a point \(x_0\) by fitting a straight line to the nearby points only, weighted so that closer points count more, and then slides this window along \(x\). The width of the window, given as the fraction of points it contains (the span), controls the flexibility and is chosen by cross-validation (ISLP Section 7.6).

    Generalised additive models (GAMs)#

    With several inputs, a generalised additive model gives each input its own shape and adds them together:

    \[ Y = \beta_0 + f_1(X_1) + f_2(X_2) + \dots + f_p(X_p) + \varepsilon . \]

    A GAM replaces each term \(\beta_j X_j\) of linear regression by a flexible function \(f_j(X_j)\) (ISLP Section 7.7). Each \(f_j\) can be a spline, a straight line or a step function, and can be plotted and interpreted on its own. The price of “additive”: a GAM misses interactions between inputs unless we add them explicitly (for example, if the air conditioning only runs during office hours).

    Building. Model the demand as a spline in the hour plus a straight line in the outdoor temperature:

    \[ \text{demand} = f_1(\text{hour}) + \beta \times (\text{outdoor temperature}) . \]
    model10-fold CV error (kW\(^2\))
    spline in hour only30.3
    spline in hour + straight line in temperature18.8
    noise variance (best possible)16

    Adding the temperature removes most of the remaining error; the GAM is close to the best possible. The fitted slope \(\hat\beta = 0.82\) kW per \(^\circ\)C (true value 0.8) has a physical meaning: each extra degree outside costs about 0.8 kW of air conditioning.
