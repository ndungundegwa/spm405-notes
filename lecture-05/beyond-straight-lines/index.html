    Beyond straight lines#

    Example 1: a building's electricity demand#

    A smart meter records the power drawn by an office building. Low at night. A morning peak (about 9 am). A larger evening peak (about 7:30 pm)

    A load profile like this is what an energy manager or a utility wants to predict, for example to size a solar system or plan tariffs. Energy monitoring was a survey topic.

    Data: 120 readings at random times. Goal: predict demand from the hour

    The relation is clearly not a straight line: it rises and falls twice.

    Polynomials and step functions#

    straight line125.6
    polynomial, degree 4112.2
    6 steps (4 h each)80.7

    Polynomial: add \(x^2, x^3, \dots, x^d\) (Lecture 2). One formula for the whole day. Low degree: too stiff for two peaks. High degree: wild near the ends

    A polynomial is global: changing the fit at 9 am changes it everywhere. A degree-4 polynomial has too few turning points to follow a night–peak–dip–peak–night shape. High degrees can follow it but swing wildly near 0 and 24 h, where data are few (ISLP Figure 7.1).

    Step function: cut the day into bins; predict the average in each bin. Simple, but jumps at the bin edges

    With 6 bins of 4 hours, each bin predicts its average demand. A peak that lies across two bins is smeared out.

    10-fold CV error (kW\(^2\)): straight line 125.6; polynomial, degree 4: 112.2; 6 steps (4 h each): 80.7. Noise variance: 16

    All three are far above the noise variance of 16, the best possible.

    Symbols

    \(d\)Polynomial degree.
    kW\(^2\)Units of squared error in demand.

    prediction (kW)17.042.036.727.054.730.7

    Hour 18.5 is in the 16–20 bin: 54.7 kW. Hour 9 is in the 8–12 bin: 36.7 kW, although the morning peak (about 50 kW at 7–8 h) is only an hour earlier: the bin edge at 8 h cuts the peak in two. This is the weakness that splines fix.

    Regression splines#

    1. Choose knots: points that cut the range into pieces. 2. Fit a cubic polynomial in each piece. 3. Force the pieces to join smoothly: same value, slope and curvature at each knot

    A cubic spline with \(K\) interior knots has \(K + 4\) coefficients (ISLP 7.4). The joining conditions make the curve look like one smooth curve.

    Still linear in the coefficients: fitted by least squares. In Python: SplineTransformer (scikit-learn)

    The spline is written as a sum of fixed basis functions (B-splines) times coefficients, so the Lecture 2 machinery applies.

    SplineTransformer turns the hour into B-spline columns; LinearRegression then fits them. n_knots counts all knots, evenly spaced over the data, including the two ends.

    Why it works: each piece only has to follow its own part of the day. No wild swings: a change in one place stays local

    Beyond the slide: natural splines

    A natural spline is also forced to be a straight line beyond the outer knots, which calms the ends (ISLP 7.4.4).

    Four fits of the same data#

    Plot: line, degree 4, steps, spline (9 knots), truth

    The dashed curve is the true demand (at 24 \(^\circ\)C outdoors).

    The straight line and the degree-4 polynomial miss both peaks. The steps are too coarse. The spline follows both peaks

    Spline, 9 knots: CV error 30.3 kW\(^2\)

    Four times smaller than the straight line. The rest (30 versus 16) is mostly the effect of outdoor temperature, which this model ignores (slide 11).

    Table: 4 knots 51.5; 5: 72.3; 6: 29.9; 9: 30.3; 12: 29.1; 25: 33.7

    Too few knots: cannot follow the peaks. Anything from 6 to 15 knots is about equally good. Too many knots: starts to fit the noise

    With few knots the result also depends on where the evenly spaced knots happen to fall relative to the peaks; that is why 5 knots does worse than 4. From 6 knots on, the pieces are short enough to follow both peaks.

    The number of knots plays the role of the polynomial degree in Lecture 4

    A flexibility setting, chosen by cross-validation.

    Two relatives#

    Smoothing spline: a knot at every data point, with a penalty on curvature: \(\mathrm{RSS} + \lambda\int g''(t)^2\,dt\). Like ridge (Lecture 4): \(\lambda\) controls how wiggly the curve may be

    \(g''(t)\) is the second derivative (curvature) of the fitted curve \(g\). The integral adds up its square over the whole range. \(\lambda = 0\) lets the curve pass through every point; \(\lambda \to \infty\) forces a straight line (ISLP 7.5).

    Local regression: to predict at \(x_0\), fit a straight line to the nearby points only, weighted by distance. Slide the window along \(x\). The window width controls the flexibility

    Also called LOESS (ISLP 7.6). The fraction of points in the window, the span, is chosen by CV.

    Students who have not met integrals recently can read \(\int\) as “add up over the whole range”.

    Symbols

    \(g(t)\)The fitted curve.
    \(g''(t)\)Its second derivative (curvature).
    \(\lambda\)Smoothing parameter.
    \(x_0\)The point where we predict.

    Generalised additive models (GAMs)#

    Several inputs, each with its own shape, added together: \(Y = \beta_0 + f_1(X_1) + f_2(X_2) + \dots + \varepsilon\)

    A GAM replaces each \(\beta_jX_j\) of linear regression by a flexible function \(f_j(X_j)\) (ISLP 7.7).

    Each \(f_j\) can be a spline, a straight line, or a step function, and can be plotted and interpreted on its own

    The price: GAMs miss interactions unless we add them (for example, if air conditioning only runs during office hours).

    Building: demand \(= f_1(\text{hour}) + \beta\,(\text{outdoor temperature})\). \(\beta\): a single slope; temperature enters as a straight line

    A spline for the daily pattern, a straight line for temperature.

    10-fold CV error: hour only (spline) 30.3; hour + temperature 18.8 (noise: 16). \(\hat\beta = 0.82\) kW per \(^\circ\)C (air conditioning)

    Adding temperature removes most of the remaining error; the GAM is close to the best possible. The temperature coefficient (true 0.8) has a physical meaning: each extra degree outside costs 0.8 kW of cooling.

    Symbols

    \(f_j\)Shape function for input \(j\).
