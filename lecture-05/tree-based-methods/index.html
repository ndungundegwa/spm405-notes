    Tree-based methods#

    A decision tree predicts by asking a short series of yes/no questions about the inputs. Trees are easy to explain and find interactions and thresholds by themselves, but a single tree is unstable. This page grows and prunes a tree, then combines many trees into the most accurate methods so far: bagging, random forests and boosting.

    Example: six sensors on each machine#

    A condition-monitoring system records six inputs for each motor:

    inputunitwhy it is measured
    vibrationmm/sworn bearings and loose mountings shake more
    bearing temperature\(^\circ\)Ca failing bearing heats up through friction
    motor currentArises when a motor is overloaded or a bearing drags
    acoustic noisedBindicates wear
    running hourshindicates wear
    humidity%included as an input that plays no part

    The output is faulty or healthy. There are 500 training machines and 5000 test machines; 27% are faulty. In the simulation, faults follow rules such as “high vibration” or “hot and high current”, but the model is not told the rules. Rules with “and” are interactions, and their thresholds are sharp; both are hard for linear models and natural for trees.

    A decision tree#

    A tree asks yes/no questions, one input at a time. Each question is a node; each end of the tree is a leaf, which gives the prediction. A classification leaf predicts the majority class of the training machines that reach it; a regression leaf predicts their average.

    Read the tree from the top. A machine with vibration 3 mm/s, temperature 70 \(^\circ\)C and current 12 A goes left (vibration \(\le 4.5\)), then right (temperature \(> 65.9\)), then right (current \(> 10.5\)): faulty. A technician can follow this without a computer.

    How a tree is grown#

    Trees are grown by recursive binary splitting:

    1. Try every input and every possible cut point.
    2. Keep the split that makes the two resulting groups most pure (each group as close as possible to a single class).
    3. Repeat inside each group, until the groups are very small or pure.

    Searching all possible trees is impossible, so this procedure is greedy: it picks the best split now, not the best tree overall. It is fast and works well in practice.

    For classification, purity is measured by the Gini index of a group,

    \[ G = \sum_k \hat p_k\,(1 - \hat p_k), \]

    where \(\hat p_k\) is the fraction of the group in class \(k\). \(G = 0\) when every machine in the group is the same class; with two classes, \(G = 0.5\) when they are half and half. (Regression trees use the RSS instead.) The quality of a split is the size-weighted average of the two groups' \(G\) values; the best split lowers it most.

    Worked numbers

    A group with 30% faulty: \(G = 0.3 \times 0.7 + 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.42\). After a split into a group with 5% faulty and one with 90% faulty: \(G = 2 \times 0.05 \times 0.95 = 0.095\) and \(G = 2 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.18\). Both are much purer.

    Example: choosing a split with the Gini index#

    A node holds 40 faulty and 60 healthy machines. Two candidate splits:

    • A (vibration > 3 mm/s): left 10 faulty / 50 healthy, right 30 faulty / 10 healthy.
    • B (temperature > 60 °C): left 25 faulty / 35 healthy, right 15 faulty / 25 healthy.

    Using the Gini index \(G = 2p(1-p)\) (two classes, \(p\) = fraction faulty), which split does the tree choose?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Parent: \(p = 0.4\), \(G = 2(0.4)(0.6) = 0.480\).

    A: left \(G = 0.278\) (60 machines), right \(G = 0.375\) (40). Weighted: \(0.6 \times 0.278 + 0.4 \times 0.375 = 0.317\). Gain \(= 0.163\).

    B: left \(G = 0.486\) (60), right \(G = 0.469\) (40). Weighted \(= 0.479\). Gain \(= 0.001\).

    The tree takes A: it purifies the children far more.

    Pruning: grow big, then cut back#

    A tree grown until its leaves are pure overfits. In our data 5% of the labels were flipped at random, and a deep tree grows extra leaves just to isolate those wrong labels. The remedy is to grow a big tree and then cut it back.

    Cost-complexity pruning chooses the sub-tree that minimises

    \[ \text{training error} + \alpha \times (\text{number of leaves}). \]

    Like the lasso penalty of Lecture 4, \(\alpha\) trades fit for simplicity; for each \(\alpha\) there is a best sub-tree of the full tree. \(\alpha\) is chosen by 10-fold cross-validation (in scikit-learn it is ccp_alpha).

    leavesdepthtest error
    full tree36110.110
    pruned (\(\alpha\) by CV)430.098

    The pruned tree, shown above, found the rules: “vibration above 4.5 mm/s” and “hot and high current” are exactly two of the rules used to simulate the faults, and its thresholds (4.5, 65.9, 10.5) are close to the true ones (4.5, 65, 11). A third rule (“old and noisy”) affects few machines and was pruned away.

    Trees: good and bad#

    GoodBad
    easy to explain and drawless accurate than the best methods
    no scaling needed; inputs of mixed typeshigh variance: a small change in the data can give a very different tree
    find interactions (“hot and high current”) by themselvesboundaries are made of steps

    Trees use only the order of the values, so scaling does not matter, and categories can be split directly. Their weakness is variance: if the first split changes, everything below it changes. The fix is to grow many trees and combine them.

    Bagging#

    Bagging (bootstrap aggregation):

    1. Draw \(B\) bootstrap samples from the training data (Lecture 4).
    2. Grow a deep, unpruned tree on each.
    3. Predict by majority vote (the class predicted by most trees) for classification, or by the average for regression.

    Each deep tree has low bias and high variance. Averaging many of them keeps the low bias and cancels much of the variance. If \(B\) independent estimates each have variance \(\sigma^2\), their average has variance \(\sigma^2/B\). Trees grown on bootstrap samples of the same data are not independent, so the reduction is smaller (see random forests below).

    Out-of-bag (OOB) error. Each bootstrap sample leaves out about a third of the rows (37%, i.e. \(e^{-1}\)). Predict each row using only the trees that did not see it, and you get a free estimate of the test error without cross-validation.

    OOB error on these machines

    The random forest's OOB error is 0.058, but its test error on 5000 new machines is 0.081. OOB estimates, like CV estimates, are noisy with only 500 training rows; here it is optimistic.

    Random forests#

    A random forest is bagging with one change: at each split, only a random subset of \(m\) inputs is considered, usually \(m \approx \sqrt p\). Here \(p = 6\), so \(m = 2\) (\(\sqrt 6 \approx 2.4\), rounded down).

    Why? In bagging every tree splits first on the strongest input (vibration), so the trees look alike and make the same mistakes. The variance of an average of \(B\) trees whose predictions have correlation \(\rho\) is

    \[ \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 \]

    (ESL Section 15.2). With many trees the second term vanishes and only \(\rho\sigma^2\) remains, so lowering the correlation \(\rho\) between trees is what helps. Forcing variety in the splits does exactly that.

    In Python: RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, max_features="sqrt"). Random forests rarely overfit as trees are added; more trees just cost time.

    Example: how many inputs at each split?#

    The six-sensor machines have \(p = 6\) inputs. (a) A random forest for classification tries \(m \approx \sqrt p\) inputs at each split: how many? (b) Bagging is a random forest with which \(m\)? (c) Why does choosing fewer inputs per split help?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    (a) \(\sqrt 6 = 2.45\), so \(m = 2\) inputs at each split.

    (b) Bagging uses all inputs at every split: \(m = p = 6\).

    (c) If one input is very strong, every bagged tree splits on it first, so the trees look alike and their errors are correlated; averaging correlated trees reduces the variance little. Forcing different inputs makes the trees differ, and averaging then cancels more of the noise.

    Boosting#

    Boosting grows small trees one after another, each correcting the ensemble so far:

    1. Fit a small tree to the data.
    2. Fit the next tree to what is still wrong, the residuals (measured minus predicted).
    3. Add it to the ensemble with a small weight \(\lambda\), the learning rate.
    4. Repeat.

    The model learns slowly, fixing its mistakes a little at a time. Unlike bagging, the trees are not independent (ISLP Algorithm 8.2; for classification the “residuals” are the gradients of the cross-entropy of Lecture 3). Note that ISLP reuses the letters \(\lambda\) and \(d\) here with new meanings.

    Boosting has three settings, all chosen by cross-validation:

    • the number of trees \(B\) (unlike random forests, boosting can overfit if \(B\) is too large);
    • the learning rate \(\lambda\), e.g. 0.05 (a smaller \(\lambda\) needs more trees);
    • the depth \(d\) of each tree, e.g. 1 or 2 (depth 2 lets each tree capture interactions between two inputs).

    Machines: the result#

    All methods scored on the 5000 test machines. Because 5% of the labels are random, the best possible error is about 0.05.

    Methodtest errorAUC
    Logistic regression0.1520.845
    Tree, full0.1100.856
    Tree, pruned0.0980.856
    Bagging (300 trees)0.0800.913
    Random forest (300 trees)0.0810.919
    Boosting (300 trees)0.0810.907
    • Logistic regression struggles: thresholds and “and” rules cannot be written as a weighted sum of the inputs.
    • One tree is better; many trees are better still: from 0.098 (one pruned tree) to 0.08, about a fifth fewer errors.
    • The three ensembles are about equal here. With only six inputs, the random forest's input sampling gives little advantage over bagging.

    How many trees? One tree has an error of 0.12–0.13; the error falls quickly over the first 10–25 trees, and after about 100 trees nothing changes.

    Which inputs matter? Variable importance#

    A forest of 300 trees cannot be read like a single tree, but it can report how much each input was used. For every split in every tree, the reduction in the Gini index is credited to the input used; the totals, scaled to 100%, are the variable importances.

    Vibration dominates: on its own it explains most faults, through the first rule. Humidity plays no part in the faults, yet scores 6%. This measure favours inputs with many possible cut points: deep trees also split on noise, and a continuous input offers many cut points. Permutation importance (shuffle one input and measure how much the test error rises) avoids this bias.

    Things to watch#

    • Splines at the ends: there are few data near the edges, and the curve can swing. Check the plot.
    • A single tree is unstable: refit it on another sample and it can change completely. Do not over-interpret one tree.
    • Importance is not causation, and some importance measures are biased. Check by dropping the input and re-running cross-validation.
    • Boosting can overfit if it runs for too many trees; tune it by cross-validation.
    • Scaling: trees need none, but splines and GAMs use the units of each input.

    Summary of Lecture 5#

    1. Splines fit smooth curves piece by piece; choose the number of knots by cross-validation.
    2. GAMs add up one shape per input and stay interpretable.
    3. Trees split the inputs with yes/no questions; prune them by cross-validation.
    4. Bagging, random forests and boosting combine many trees for much better accuracy.
    5. Variable importance shows which inputs a forest uses.
