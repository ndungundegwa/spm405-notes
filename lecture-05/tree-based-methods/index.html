    Tree-based methods#

    Example 2: six sensors on each machine#

    inputunit
    vibrationmm/s
    bearing temperature\(^\circ\)C
    motor currentA
    acoustic noisedB
    running hoursh
    humidity%

    Inputs: vibration (mm/s), bearing temperature (\(^\circ\)C), motor current (A), acoustic noise (dB), running hours (h), humidity (%)

    A typical condition-monitoring record. Motor current rises when a motor is overloaded or a bearing drags. Acoustic noise and running hours indicate wear. Humidity is included as an input that plays no part.

    Output: faulty or healthy. Data: 500 training machines, 5000 test machines; 27% faulty

    Faults follow rules, like “high vibration” or “hot and high current”. The model is not told the rules

    Rules with “and” are interactions; thresholds are sharp. Both are hard for linear models and natural for trees.

    A decision tree#

    Diagram: vibration \(\le 4.5\)? yes: temperature \(\le 65.9\)? yes: healthy; no: current \(\le 10.5\)? yes: healthy; no: faulty. Vibration \(> 4.5\): faulty

    Read from the top. A machine with vibration 3 mm/s, temperature 70 \(^\circ\)C and current 12 A goes left (vibration \(\le 4.5\)), right (temperature \(> 65.9\)), right (current \(> 10.5\)): faulty.

    A tree asks yes/no questions, one input at a time. Each question is a node. Each end (leaf) gives a prediction

    A classification leaf predicts its majority class; a regression leaf predicts its average.

    Easy to explain: a technician can follow it without a computer

    The tree fitted to the 500 machines (pruned; see “Pruning”)

    Slide 15 shows how it was pruned.

    How a tree is grown#

    Recursive binary splitting: 1. Try every input and every cut point. 2. Keep the split that makes the two groups most pure. 3. Repeat inside each group

    Each split divides one group of machines in two. The process stops when groups are very small or pure.

    Purity for classification: the Gini index \(G = \sum_k \hat p_k(1 - \hat p_k)\). Regression trees use the RSS instead

    Gini index: \(G = 0\): every machine in the group is the same class. \(G = 0.5\): half and half (two classes)

    Searching all possible trees is impossible; greedy splitting is fast and works well in practice.

    Symbols

    \(\hat p_k\)Fraction of the machines in the group that are in class \(k\).
    \(G\)Gini index of the group.

    Worked numbers

    A group with 30% faulty: \(G = 0.3 \times 0.7 + 0.7 \times 0.3 = 0.42\). After a split into a group with 5% faulty and one with 90% faulty: \(G = 2 \times 0.05 \times 0.95 = 0.095\) and \(G = 2 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.18\). Both are much purer. The split's quality is the size-weighted average of the two \(G\) values.

    Example: choosing a split with the Gini index#

    A node holds 40 faulty and 60 healthy machines. Two candidate splits:

    • A (vibration > 3 mm/s): left 10 faulty / 50 healthy, right 30 faulty / 10 healthy.
    • B (temperature > 60 °C): left 25 faulty / 35 healthy, right 15 faulty / 25 healthy.

    Using the Gini index \(G = 2p(1-p)\), which split does the tree choose?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Parent: \(p = 0.4\), \(G = 2(0.4)(0.6) = 0.480\).

    A: left \(G = 0.278\) (60 machines), right \(G = 0.375\) (40). Weighted: \(0.6 \times 0.278 + 0.4 \times 0.375 = 0.317\). Gain \(= 0.163\).

    B: left \(G = 0.486\) (60), right \(G = 0.469\) (40). Weighted \(= 0.479\). Gain \(= 0.001\).

    The tree takes A: it purifies the children far more.

    Pruning: grow big, then cut back#

    leavesdepthtest error
    full tree36110.110
    pruned (CV)430.098

    Table: full tree 36 leaves, depth 11, test error 0.110; pruned (CV) 4 leaves, depth 3, test error 0.098

    Depth is the number of questions on the longest path from the top to a leaf.

    Cost-complexity pruning: minimise error \(+ \alpha \times\) (number of leaves). Choose \(\alpha\) by 10-fold cross-validation

    Like the lasso penalty (Lecture 4), \(\alpha\) trades fit for simplicity. For each \(\alpha\) there is a best sub-tree of the full tree.

    The full tree overfits: 36 leaves fit the noise in 500 machines

    With 5% of labels flipped at random, a deep tree grows leaves to isolate the wrong labels.

    The pruned tree found the rules: “vibration above 4.5 mm/s” and “hot and high current” are exactly two of the rules used to simulate the faults

    The fitted thresholds (4.5, 65.9, 10.5) are close to the true ones (4.5, 65, 11). The third rule (old and noisy) affects few machines and was pruned away.

    Symbols

    \(\alpha\)Pruning penalty per leaf (ccp_alpha in scikit-learn).

    Trees: good and bad#

    GoodBad
    Easy to explain and drawLess accurate than the best methods
    No scaling needed; mixed input typesHigh variance: a small change in the data can give a very different tree
    Find interactions (“hot and high current”) by themselvesBoundaries are made of steps

    Good: easy to explain and draw; no scaling needed; mixed input types; find interactions by themselves

    Trees use only the order of the values, so scaling does not matter, and categories can be split directly.

    Bad: less accurate than the best methods; high variance: a small change in the data can give a very different tree; boundaries are made of steps

    If the first split changes, everything below it changes.

    Fix the variance by growing many trees and combining them

    Bagging#

    Bootstrap aggregation: 1. Draw \(B\) bootstrap samples (Lecture 4). 2. Grow a deep tree on each. 3. Predict by majority vote (classification) or average (regression)

    Each deep tree has low bias and high variance. Averaging many of them keeps the low bias and reduces the variance.

    Averaging \(B\) noisy trees cancels much of their variance

    If \(B\) independent estimates each have variance \(\sigma^2\), their average has variance \(\sigma^2/B\). Trees from bootstrap samples are not independent, so the reduction is smaller (next slide).

    Out-of-bag (OOB) error: each tree never sees about a third of the rows. Predicting those rows gives a free estimate of the test error, without CV

    Each row is out of bag for about 37% of the trees (Lecture 4, \(e^{-1}\)); its OOB prediction is the vote of those trees.

    Symbols

    \(B\)Number of bootstrap samples (trees).
    Beyond the slide: OOB error on these machines

    The random forest's OOB error is 0.058, but its test error on 5000 new machines is 0.081. OOB estimates, like CV estimates, are noisy with only 500 training rows; here it is optimistic (notebook question 7).

    Random forests#

    Bagging, with one change: at each split, consider only a random subset of \(m\) inputs, usually \(m \approx \sqrt p\). Here \(p = 6\) inputs, so \(m = 2\) (\(\sqrt 6 \approx 2.4\))

    \(\sqrt 6 = 2.4\), rounded down to 2.

    Why? In bagging, every tree splits first on the strongest input (vibration), so the trees look alike. Forcing variety makes the trees less correlated, and their average more stable

    The variance of an average of correlated trees is \(\rho\sigma^2 + (1 - \rho)\sigma^2/B\) (ESL 15.2): with many trees, only the correlation term \(\rho\sigma^2\) remains, so lowering \(\rho\) is what helps.

    In Python: RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, max_features=“sqrt”)

    Random forests rarely overfit as trees are added; more trees just cost time

    Symbols

    \(m\)Number of inputs tried at each split.
    \(\rho\)Correlation between two trees' predictions.

    Example: how many inputs at each split?#

    The six-sensor machines have \(p = 6\) inputs. (a) A random forest for classification tries \(m \approx \sqrt p\) inputs at each split: how many? (b) Bagging is a random forest with which \(m\)? (c) Why does choosing fewer inputs per split help?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    (a) \(\sqrt 6 = 2.45\), so \(m = 2\) inputs at each split.

    (b) Bagging uses all inputs at every split: \(m = p = 6\).

    (c) If one input is very strong, every bagged tree splits on it first, so the trees look alike and their errors are correlated; averaging correlated trees reduces the variance little. Forcing different inputs makes the trees differ, and averaging then cancels more of the noise.

    Boosting#

    Grow small trees one after another: 1. Fit a small tree to the data. 2. Fit the next tree to what is still wrong (the residuals). 3. Add it with a small weight \(\lambda\) (the learning rate). 4. Repeat

    Unlike bagging, the trees are not independent: each one corrects the ensemble so far (ISLP Algorithm 8.2; for classification the “residuals” are gradients of the cross-entropy).

    The model learns slowly, fixing its mistakes a little at a time

    Note both clashes of letters explicitly: ISLP reuses \(\lambda\) and \(d\) here.

    Three settings: number of trees \(B\) (can overfit if too large); learning rate \(\lambda\) (e.g. 0.05); tree depth \(d\) (e.g. 1 or 2)

    Depth 2 lets each tree capture two-input interactions. A smaller \(\lambda\) needs more trees.

    Choose them by cross-validation

    All on 5000 test machines. The floor is 0.05 because 5% of labels are random.

    Logistic regression struggles: the rules are not straight lines

    Thresholds and “and” rules cannot be written as a weighted sum of the inputs.

    One tree is better; many trees are better still. The three ensembles are about equal here

    From 0.098 (one pruned tree) to 0.08: about a fifth fewer errors. With only six inputs, the random forest's input sampling gives little advantage over bagging.

    How many trees?#

    One tree: error 0.12–0.13. The error falls quickly over the first 10–25 trees. After about 100 trees nothing changes

    From the notebook: random forest 0.135 with 1 tree, 0.084 with 25, 0.081 with 300; bagging 0.123, 0.078, 0.080.

    Which inputs matter? Variable importance#

    Bar chart: vibration 57, temperature 13, current 9, noise 8, hours 7, humidity 6 (%)

    Random forest: how much each input reduces the Gini index (impurity), added over all splits and trees, as % of the total

    Every split lowers the Gini index; the reductions are credited to the input used, summed over the forest and scaled to 100%.

    Vibration dominates

    It alone explains most faults, through the first rule.

    Humidity plays no part in the faults, yet scores 6%: this measure favours inputs with many possible cut points

    Deep trees also split on noise, and a continuous input offers many cut points. Permutation importance (shuffle one input and measure how much the test error rises) avoids this bias.

    Things to watch#

    Splines at the ends: few data near the edges; the curve can swing. Check the plot

    A single tree is unstable: refit on another sample and it can change completely. Do not over-interpret one tree

    Importance is not causation, and some importance measures are biased. Check by dropping the input and re-running CV

    Boosting can overfit if it runs for too many trees; tune by CV

    Trees need no scaling, but splines and GAMs do use the units of each input

    Summary#

    1. Splines fit smooth curves piece by piece; choose the number of knots by CV
    1. GAMs add up one shape per input and stay interpretable
    1. Trees split the inputs with yes/no questions; prune them by CV
    1. Bagging, random forests and boosting combine many trees for much better accuracy
    1. Variable importance shows which inputs the forest uses
