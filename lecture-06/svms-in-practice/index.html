    SVMs in practice#

    This page covers SVMs with more than two classes, explains why SVMs and logistic regression so often give similar answers, and lists the practical points to watch.

    Notation on this page

    SymbolMeaning
    \(K\)number of classes
    \(f(x_i)\)the SVM's (or logistic regression's) linear score for point \(i\); its sign gives the class
    \(y_i\)class of point \(i\), coded \(+1\) or \(-1\)
    \(y_i f(x_i)\)signed distance: positive on the correct side, negative on the wrong side, large when far on the correct side
    hinge lossthe SVM's loss, \(\max(0,\ 1 - y_i f(x_i))\): zero outside the street, rising in a straight line inside it
    log lossthe loss of logistic regression (the cross-entropy of Lecture 3)

    More than two classes#

    An SVM separates two classes. For \(K\) classes there are two standard schemes:

    • One-versus-one: fit an SVM for every pair of classes, \(K(K - 1)/2\) SVMs in all. Each SVM votes, and the class with the most votes wins. scikit-learn's SVC does this.
    • One-versus-all: fit \(K\) SVMs, each separating one class from all the others, and choose the class whose SVM is most confident (largest \(f(x)\)).

    For the fault type (imbalance, misalignment or bearing wear, \(K = 3\)) both need 3 SVMs (\(3 \times 2/2 = 3\) pairs). With 10 classes it would be 45 against 10.

    SVMs and logistic regression#

    An SVM can be written as minimising a loss plus a ridge-like penalty on the coefficients (ISLP Section 9.5). Its loss is the hinge loss

    \[ \max\bigl(0,\ 1 - y_i f(x_i)\bigr), \]

    where \(y_i f(x_i)\) is the signed distance of point \(i\): positive for a correctly classified point, and large when the point is far on the correct side. (The name comes from the shape of its graph, bent like a door hinge at \(y_i f(x_i) = 1\).)

    The two losses have almost the same shape:

    • Both penalise points on the wrong side, roughly in proportion to how far they are.
    • The SVM ignores points safely outside the street (loss exactly 0). That is why only the support vectors matter. The log loss is never exactly zero, so every point has a little influence.

    So the two methods usually give similar answers: on milk data set B the test errors were 0.177 (best SVM) and 0.176 (logistic regression). The practical difference is in the output: logistic regression gives probabilities; an SVM gives a class (and a distance from the boundary).

    Things to watch#

    • Standardise the inputs: kernels use distances.
    • Tune \(C\) and \(\gamma\) together by cross-validation, over a grid. They interact: a large \(\gamma\) needs a smaller \(C\) to avoid overfitting.
    • Know your library's \(C\): scikit-learn's \(C\) works in the opposite direction to ISLP's.
    • Large data sets: kernel SVMs become slow beyond some tens of thousands of rows; training time grows roughly with the square of the number of rows, or worse.
    • Probabilities: SVC(probability=True) estimates them with an extra step (fitting a sigmoid to the SVM distances by internal cross-validation, called Platt scaling). They are less reliable than those of logistic regression.

    Summary of Lecture 6#

    1. The maximal margin classifier finds the widest street between two classes.
    2. Only the support vectors decide the boundary.
    3. The soft margin allows overlap; \(C\) controls the width of the street (in scikit-learn, small \(C\) = wide).
    4. Kernels give curved boundaries by enlarging the feature space implicitly.
    5. SVMs and logistic regression often perform alike; kernels win when the boundary is curved.
