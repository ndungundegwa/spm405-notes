    The maximal margin classifier#

    Trees cut the input space into boxes. Support vector machines take a different view of classification: look for the widest possible gap between the classes, and, if needed, bend that gap into a curve. This page covers the first step, for classes that a straight line can separate completely: hyperplanes, the margin, and support vectors.

    Example: has water been added to the milk?#

    Adding water to milk changes two easily measured properties:

    • it lowers the density: water (1.000 g/mL) is lighter than milk (about 1.030 g/mL), so each 10% of added water lowers the density by about 0.003 g/mL;
    • it lowers the electrical conductivity, because milk's conductivity comes from dissolved salts, and water dilutes them.

    Both are measured with cheap instruments: a lactometer (a float, calibrated for milk, that reads its density; used at collection centres) and a conductivity probe. The output is pure or adulterated.

    We use two data sets:

    • A: 30 samples in which the watered samples have so much water added that the classes separate completely. Used on this page.
    • B: 200 samples in which the classes overlap. Used on the next page.

    Both inputs are standardised (mean 0, standard deviation 1). Density varies by thousandths and conductivity by tenths; without standardising, every distance would be dominated by conductivity, and SVMs are built on distances.

    A hyperplane#

    With two inputs, a hyperplane is a straight line:

    \[ \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \beta_2 X_2 = 0 . \]

    With \(p\) inputs it is a flat surface of dimension \(p - 1\): a line in two dimensions, an ordinary plane in three, and so on.

    A hyperplane classifies by the sign of \(f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2\): points with \(f(x) > 0\) belong to one class, points with \(f(x) < 0\) to the other. The line divides the plane into two halves.

    The size of \(f(x)\) tells the confidence: the farther a point is from the line, the surer we are of its class. If the coefficients are scaled so that \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 = 1\), then \(f(x)\) is exactly the signed distance from the line.

    Example: which side of the hyperplane?#

    A classifier for milk uses the hyperplane \(-3 + x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\), with \(x_1\) the conductivity and \(x_2\) the density reading (both standardised); positive values mean “water added”. Classify the samples \((1, 0.5)\), \((2, 1.0)\) and \((3, 1.5)\).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Evaluate \(f(x) = -3 + x_1 + 2x_2\):

    • \((1, 0.5)\): \(f = -1.0\): pure.
    • \((2, 1.0)\): \(f = +1.0\): water added.
    • \((3, 1.5)\): \(f = +3.0\): water added.

    The size of \(f\) also matters: the further from 0, the further the sample lies from the boundary, and the more confident the classification.

    If the classes can be separated by a line at all, there are infinitely many separating lines. Which one should we choose?

    The widest street#

    The margin \(M\) of a separating line is the distance from the line to the nearest data point. The maximal margin classifier chooses the line with the largest margin: the widest “street” between the two classes, with the line down its middle.

    The points that lie on the edges of the street are the support vectors. They alone fix the line; they “support” the street, like posts. Why the widest street? A new sample that lands near the boundary is then less likely to fall on the wrong side.

    Finding the line is an optimisation problem (ISLP Section 9.1.4). Code the classes as \(y_i = +1\) and \(y_i = -1\). Then

    \[ \text{maximise } M \quad \text{subject to} \quad \beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 = 1 \quad\text{and}\quad y_i\,(\beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \beta_2x_{i2}) \ge M \ \text{ for every } i . \]

    The constraint says that every point is on its correct side (\(y_i f(x_i) > 0\)) and at least a distance \(M\) from the line.

    Another way of writing it, used by scikit-learn: write the line as \(w \cdot x + b = 0\) (with \(w = (w_1, w_2)\) the coefficients and \(b\) the intercept), and scale \(w\) and \(b\) so that the edges of the street are \(w \cdot x + b = \pm 1\). The distance between these two parallel lines, the street width, is \(2/\lVert w\rVert\), where \(\lVert w\rVert = \sqrt{w_1^2 + w_2^2}\). Maximising the width is the same as minimising \(\lVert w\rVert\).

    Example: the width of the street#

    A maximal margin classifier is written \(\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x = 0\) with the support vectors on \(\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x = \pm 1\). If \(\boldsymbol\beta = (3, 4)\), how wide is the margin (the street between the two classes)?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The distance between the parallel lines \(\boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x + \beta_0 = +1\) and \(= -1\) is \(2/\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\):

    \[ \|\boldsymbol\beta\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5, \qquad \text{width} = 2/5 = 0.4. \]

    The SVM maximises this width, which is the same as minimising \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\).

    Data set A: the widest street#

    scikit-learn finds \(w = (-0.966, -0.664)\) and \(b = -0.318\).

    • 3 support vectors out of 30 samples: two on one edge and one on the other, the minimum needed to fix a line in two dimensions.
    • Street width 1.71 standardised units (standard deviations), from \(2/\lVert w\rVert\).
    • Only the support vectors matter. Move any other point, without letting it cross into the street, and the line does not change. This is very different from logistic regression, where every point pulls on the fit.

    Worked numbers

    \(\lVert w\rVert = \sqrt{0.966^2 + 0.664^2} = \sqrt{0.933 + 0.441} = 1.172\); width \(= 2/1.172 = 1.71\).
