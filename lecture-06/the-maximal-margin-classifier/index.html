    The maximal margin classifier#

    Example: has water been added to the milk?#

    Adding water to milk lowers its density (g/mL); lowers its electrical conductivity (mS/cm), because the salts are diluted

    Water (1.000 g/mL) is lighter than milk (about 1.030), so each 10% of added water lowers the density by about 0.003 g/mL. Milk's conductivity comes from dissolved salts; diluting them lowers it. Milk adulteration was named in the class survey.

    Two inputs, both measured with cheap instruments (a lactometer and a conductivity probe). Output: pure or adulterated

    A lactometer is a hydrometer calibrated for milk, used at collection centres.

    Two datasets: A: 30 samples, classes clearly apart (used first). B: 200 samples, classes overlap (used for the soft margin)

    In A the watered samples have more water added, so the classes separate completely. A is used on slide 8, B on slides 11–12.

    Inputs are standardised: mean 0, standard deviation 1

    Density varies by thousandths, conductivity by tenths; without standardising, distances would be dominated by conductivity.

    A hyperplane#

    With two inputs, a hyperplane is a straight line: \(\beta_0 + \beta_1X_1 + \beta_2X_2 = 0\). With \(p\) inputs it is a flat surface of dimension \(p - 1\)

    In three dimensions a hyperplane is an ordinary plane.

    Classify by the sign: \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_2x_2 > 0\): one class; \(< 0\): the other

    The line divides the plane into two half-planes.

    Size tells confidence: the farther a point is from the line, the more sure we are of its class

    If \(\beta_1^2 + \beta_2^2 = 1\), the value \(\beta_0 + \beta_1x_1 + \beta_2x_2\) is exactly the signed distance from the line.

    If the classes can be separated at all, there are infinitely many separating lines. Which one?

    Symbols

    \(\beta_0, \beta_1, \beta_2\)Coefficients of the line.
    \(X_1, X_2\)The two inputs (standardised density and conductivity).

    Example: which side of the hyperplane?#

    A classifier for milk uses the hyperplane \(-3 + x_1 + 2x_2 = 0\), with \(x_1\) the conductivity and \(x_2\) the density reading (both standardised); positive values mean “water added”. Classify the samples \((1, 0.5)\), \((2, 1.0)\) and \((3, 1.5)\).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Evaluate \(f(x) = -3 + x_1 + 2x_2\):

    • \((1, 0.5)\): \(f = -1.0\): pure.
    • \((2, 1.0)\): \(f = +1.0\): water added.
    • \((3, 1.5)\): \(f = +3.0\): water added.

    The size of \(f\) also matters: the further from 0, the further the sample lies from the boundary, and the more confident the classification.

    The maximal margin classifier#

    Margin: the distance from the line to the nearest point. Choose the line with the largest margin: the widest “street” between the classes

    Support vectors: the points on the edge of the street. They alone fix the line

    They “support” the street, like posts.

    Why the widest street? A new sample near the boundary is less likely to fall on the wrong side

    Solved as an optimisation problem: find the \(\beta\)'s that make the margin as large as possible (ISLP 9.1.4)

    Maximise \(M\) subject to \(\sum_j\beta_j^2 = 1\) and \(y_i(\beta_0 + \beta_1x_{i1} + \dots) \ge M\) for every \(i\), with \(y_i = \pm1\).

    Symbols

    \(M\)Margin: half the street width.
    \(y_i\)Class of point \(i\) coded as \(+1\) or \(-1\).

    Example: the width of the street#

    A maximal margin classifier is written \(\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x = 0\) with the support vectors on \(\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x = \pm 1\). If \(\boldsymbol\beta = (3, 4)\), how wide is the margin (the street between the two classes)?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The distance between the parallel lines \(\boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x + \beta_0 = +1\) and \(= -1\) is \(2/\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\):

    \[ \|\boldsymbol\beta\| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5, \qquad \text{width} = 2/5 = 0.4. \]

    The SVM maximises this width, which is the same as minimising \(\|\boldsymbol\beta\|\).

    Dataset A: the widest street#

    Solid: the maximal margin line. Dashed: the edges of the street

    scikit-learn writes the line as \(w_1z_1 + w_2z_2 + b = 0\) and the edges as \(= \pm1\). Here \(w = (-0.966, -0.664)\), \(b = -0.318\).

    3 support vectors (circled) out of 30 samples

    Two on one edge and one on the other, the minimum needed to fix a line in two dimensions.

    Street width: 1.71 (standardised units, i.e. standard deviations)

    The distance between the two edges is \(2/\Vert w\Vert \).

    Move any other point (without crossing the street): the line does not change

    This is different from logistic regression, where every point pulls on the fit. Notebook question 1 tests it.

    Symbols

    \(w\), \(b\)scikit-learn's coefficients and intercept.
    \(\Vert w\Vert \)Length of \(w\), \(\sqrt{w_1^2 + w_2^2}\).

    Worked numbers

    \(\Vert w\Vert = \sqrt{0.966^2 + 0.664^2} = \sqrt{0.933 + 0.441} = 1.172\); width \(= 2/1.172 = 1.71\).
