    The support vector classifier#

    Real data overlap: usually no straight line separates the classes perfectly, and then the maximal margin classifier of the previous page has no solution. The support vector classifier allows some points inside the street, or even on the wrong side, at a price set by a cost parameter \(C\).

    SymbolRead asMeaning
    violationa point inside the street or on the wrong side of the line
    \(\varepsilon_i\)“epsilon i”slack of point \(i\): how far it violates the margin

    A soft margin#

    The support vector classifier, also called the soft margin classifier, allows some points inside the street or on the wrong side of the line, but makes us pay for each such violation. Each point gets a slack variable \(\varepsilon_i\) (ISLP Section 9.2.2):

    • \(\varepsilon_i = 0\): the point is on the correct side, outside the street;
    • \(0 < \varepsilon_i \le 1\): the point is inside the street, but on the correct side of the line;
    • \(\varepsilon_i > 1\): the point is on the wrong side of the line (misclassified).

    The support vectors are now all points on the edge of the street, inside it, or on the wrong side. Again, only they decide the line.

    A soft margin often helps even when the data can be separated. Because only the support vectors matter, a single unusual sample close to the other class can swing a hard-margin line; with a soft margin, one odd sample no longer decides the boundary.

    The cost parameter \(C\): two opposite conventions#

    The amount of violation allowed is controlled by a parameter \(C\). Unfortunately, the textbook and the software use \(C\) in opposite directions.

    • ISLP: \(C\) is a budget. The total slack must satisfy \(\sum_i \varepsilon_i \le C\) (ISLP equation 9.15): \(C\) is how much violation we can afford.
    • scikit-learn: \(C\) is a penalty. It minimises \(\tfrac12\lVert w\rVert^2 + C\sum_i \varepsilon_i\): \(C\) is the price per unit of violation.
    ISLP: \(C\) is a budgetscikit-learn: \(C\) is a penalty
    large \(C\)many violations allowed: wide streetviolations are expensive: narrow street
    small \(C\)few violations allowed: narrow streetviolations are cheap: wide street

    Same idea, opposite direction. These notes use the scikit-learn convention: a small \(C\) gives a wide street with more support vectors. A wide street is decided by many points, so it changes little between data sets (low variance), but it may not follow the data closely (high bias). Reading “large \(C\) gives a wide margin” in ISLP and applying it in scikit-learn gives the opposite of what was intended, so always check which convention a library uses.

    Data set B: the effect of \(C\)#

    Data set B has 200 training samples and 2000 test samples of milk, with overlapping classes.

    0.011502.770.207

    100670.890.176
    • Small \(C\): a wide street, so wide that 150 of the 200 points are inside it and are support vectors.
    • From \(C = 1\) upwards the answer hardly changes. Once violations are expensive enough, the line is set by the points in the overlap region, and making violations more expensive changes nothing.
    • Logistic regression on the same data has test error 0.176, equal to the best SVM. The reason is explained on the SVMs in practice page.

    As with \(\lambda\) in Lecture 4, \(C\) is a hyperparameter, chosen by cross-validation.

    What cross-validation says here

    The 10-fold CV errors are 0.135, 0.150, 0.160, 0.160 and 0.165 for \(C = 0.01\) to 100: CV would choose \(C = 0.01\), which is the worst on the test data. With 200 samples, one misclassification changes a CV error by 0.005, so these differences are within the noise. The lesson of Lecture 4 applies: small differences in CV error are not meaningful.
