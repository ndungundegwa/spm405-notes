    Clustering#

    Clustering looks for groups of similar objects in data that carry no labels. This page covers the two standard methods, \(k\)-means and hierarchical clustering, using the daily electricity profiles of a utility's customers, and shows why the choice of scaling decides what the clusters will be.

    Notation on this page

    SymbolMeaning
    profilea customer's energy use in each of the 24 hours of a typical day (24 numbers)
    \(k\)number of clusters, chosen by us
    centrethe average of all the profiles in a cluster
    distancebetween two profiles \(x\) and \(x'\): \(\sqrt{\sum_j (x_j - x'_j)^2}\), summed over the 24 hours (as in KNN)
    within-cluster variationtotal squared distance from each customer to its cluster centre
    linkagehow the distance between two clusters is defined in hierarchical clustering
    dendrogramthe tree diagram that records every merge in hierarchical clustering
    adjusted Rand indexagreement between two groupings: 1 = identical, 0 = no better than chance

    Example: electricity customers#

    A utility has smart-meter data for 300 customers: the energy each one uses in each hour of a typical day, 24 numbers per customer. Are there groups of customers with similar daily patterns? Time-of-use tariffs and the planning of transformers both depend on when customers draw power.

    We care about the shape of the profile, not its size, so each profile is divided by the customer's daily total. Each profile then becomes 24 percentages adding up to 100. (What happens if we skip this step is shown below.)

    \(k\)-means clustering#

    Choose the number of clusters \(k\). Then (ISLP Algorithm 12.2):

    1. Give each customer a random cluster.
    2. Compute each cluster's centre: the average profile of its members.
    3. Move each customer to the cluster whose centre is nearest (smallest distance over the 24 hourly values).
    4. Repeat steps 2–3 until nothing changes.

    \(k\)-means minimises the within-cluster variation: the total squared distance from each customer to its cluster centre. Each repetition of steps 2–3 can only lower it, so the algorithm always stops. But it stops at a local minimum, which depends on the random start, so in practice it is run many times from different starts and the best result is kept (in scikit-learn, n_init=20).

    Example: k-means by hand#

    Daily energy use of six customers (MWh): 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 12. Run \(k\)-means with \(k = 2\) starting from centres 1 and 8 until nothing changes.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Assign: nearest centre: \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) to 1, \(\{7, 8, 12\}\) to 8. Move: centres become 2.00 and 9.00.

    Assign again: \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) and \(\{7, 8, 12\}\). Move: centres 2.00 and 9.00.

    Another assignment changes nothing, so the algorithm stops: two groups, small users and large users.

    How many clusters? The elbow#

    More clusters always lower the within-cluster variation: with \(k = n\) every customer is its own cluster and the variation is zero, which says nothing. So plot the variation against \(k\) and look for the elbow.

    Going from \(k = 1\) to 2 cuts the variation to 19%, and from 2 to 3 to 9%; beyond 3 there is hardly any gain. Choose \(k\) at the elbow, here \(k = 3\), and then check that the clusters make sense.

    Three types of customer#

    • Blue (180 customers): an evening peak at 8 pm and a small morning peak: households.
    • Orange (99): daytime use, 8 am to 5 pm: offices and shops.
    • Red (21): night-time use: night businesses.

    We named the clusters; the algorithm only found them. Here they match the true customer types exactly (adjusted Rand index 1.00, defined below). Real data are rarely this clean.

    Scaling changes the answer#

    Run \(k\)-means on the raw kWh instead of the shapes, and the clusters (159, 75 and 66 customers) group customers by how much they use, not when. Big customers differ from small ones by more kWh in every hour, and that dominates the distances. Agreement with the true types drops from 1.00 to 0.29.

    The agreement is measured by the adjusted Rand index, which compares two groupings while ignoring the cluster numbers: 1 means identical groups, 0 means no better than chance. It needs the true labels, so it is only available when checking on simulated or labelled data.

    The lesson: decide what “similar” means. Scaling, units and the choice of variables decide what the clusters will be. There is no “correct” answer without a purpose: for sizing transformers, grouping by amount could be exactly what is wanted.

    Example: why scale before clustering?#

    Two customers are described by (number of occupants, monthly energy in kWh): A = (2, 300) and B = (3, 310). Compute their Euclidean distance as it stands, then after dividing each variable by its standard deviation (0.5 occupants and 40 kWh). Which variable dominates each time?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Raw: \(\sqrt{(3-2)^2 + (310-300)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 100} = 10.05\): the energy difference dominates only because kWh are large numbers.

    Scaled: differences \(1/0.5 = 2\) and \(10/40 = 0.25\): \(\sqrt{4 + 0.0625} = 2.02\). Now the occupants dominate: one extra person is a big change compared with the spread of occupants, while 10 kWh is small compared with the spread of energy. Clusters depend on this choice.

    Hierarchical clustering#

    Hierarchical clustering does not need \(k\) in advance (ISLP Algorithm 12.3):

    1. Start with each customer as its own cluster.
    2. Join the two closest clusters.
    3. Repeat until one cluster is left.

    The distance between two clusters is defined by the linkage:

    • complete linkage: the largest distance between their members (their farthest pair);
    • average linkage: the average distance between their members;
    • single linkage: the smallest distance between their members.

    The result is drawn as a dendrogram (tree diagram) that records every join; the height of each join is the distance between the clusters joined. Cutting the tree across at some height gives the clusters.

    All three linkages recover the three types exactly on these data.

    Practical issues#

    • Scale the variables to match your purpose (see above).
    • Choose the linkage. Complete and average linkage usually give balanced clusters; single linkage tends to chain points into long, straggly clusters.
    • Clustering always finds clusters, even in random data. Check that they are stable (re-run on subsets of the data) and meaningful.
    • Outliers can form their own clusters or distort the centres.
    • Report clusters as a starting point for investigation, not as the final truth (ISLP Section 12.4.3).
