    Clustering#

    Example: electricity customers#

    A utility has smart-meter data for 300 customers: the energy used in each hour of a typical day (24 numbers)

    Question: are there groups of customers with similar daily patterns? (For tariffs, or for planning the network.)

    Time-of-use tariffs and transformer planning both depend on when customers draw power.

    We care about the shape, not the size: divide each profile (the 24 hourly values) by the customer's daily total

    Each profile becomes 24 percentages adding up to 100. Slide 16 shows what happens otherwise.

    Plot: three customers. The types are not labelled

    \(k\)-means clustering#

    Choose the number of clusters \(k\). Then: 1. Give each customer a random cluster. 2. Compute each cluster's centre (the average profile). 3. Move each customer to the nearest centre. 4. Repeat 2–3 until nothing changes

    ISLP Algorithm 12.2. Each repetition can only lower the within-cluster variation, so the algorithm always stops.

    What it minimises: the total squared distance from each customer to its cluster centre (within-cluster variation)

    The answer depends on the random start: run it many times and keep the best (n_init=20)

    It finds a local, not necessarily global, minimum.

    Example: k-means by hand#

    Daily energy use of six customers (MWh): 1, 2, 3, 7, 8, 12. Run \(k\)-means with \(k = 2\) starting from centres 1 and 8 until nothing changes.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Assign: nearest centre: \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) to 1, \(\{7, 8, 12\}\) to 8. Move: centres become 2.00 and 9.00.

    Assign again: \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) and \(\{7, 8, 12\}\). Move: centres 2.00 and 9.00.

    Another assignment changes nothing, so the algorithm stops: two groups, small users and large users.

    How many clusters? The elbow#

    Plot: within-cluster variation against \(k\), as % of the value for \(k = 1\)

    \(k = 1 \to 2\): falls to 19%. \(k = 2 \to 3\): falls to 9%. Beyond 3: hardly any gain

    From the notebook: 100, 18.9, 8.9, 8.2, 7.8% for \(k = 1\) to 5.

    More clusters always lower the variation. Choose \(k\) at the elbow, and check that the clusters make sense

    With \(k = n\), every customer is its own cluster and the variation is zero; that says nothing.

    Three types of customer#

    Plot: the three cluster centres (\(k\)-means, \(k = 3\)): 180 customers, 99, 21

    Blue: evening peak at 8 pm, small morning peak: households. Orange: daytime, 8 am to 5 pm: offices and shops. Red: night: night businesses

    We named the clusters; the algorithm only found them. Here they match the true types exactly

    The adjusted Rand index between the clusters and the true types is 1.00: every customer is in the right group. Real data are rarely this clean.

    Scaling changes the answer#

    \(k\)-means on the raw kWh instead of the shapes: clusters of 159, 75 and 66 customers; they group customers by how much they use, not when; agreement with the true types: 0.29 (shapes: 1.00)

    Big customers differ from small ones by more kWh in every hour, which dominates the distances.

    Decide what “similar” means: scaling, units and the choice of variables decide what the clusters will be. There is no “correct” answer without a purpose

    For sizing transformers, grouping by amount could be exactly what is wanted.

    Agreement measured by the adjusted Rand index: 1 = identical groups, 0 = no better than chance

    It compares two groupings, ignoring the cluster numbers. It needs the true labels, so it is only available for checking on simulated or labelled data.

    Example: why scale before clustering?#

    Two customers are described by (number of occupants, monthly energy in kWh): A = (2, 300) and B = (3, 310). Compute their Euclidean distance as it stands, then after dividing each variable by its standard deviation (0.5 occupants and 40 kWh). Which variable dominates each time?

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Raw: \(\sqrt{(3-2)^2 + (310-300)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 100} = 10.05\): the energy difference dominates only because kWh are large numbers.

    Scaled: differences \(1/0.5 = 2\) and \(10/40 = 0.25\): \(\sqrt{4 + 0.0625} = 2.02\). Now the occupants dominate: one extra person is a big change compared with the spread of occupants, while 10 kWh is small compared with the spread of energy. Clusters depend on this choice.

    Hierarchical clustering#

    Dendrogram: 40 customers, complete linkage. Dots: true type

    1. Start with each customer as its own cluster. 2. Join the two closest clusters. 3. Repeat until one cluster is left

    ISLP Algorithm 12.3. The distance between two clusters is defined by the linkage: complete (largest distance between their members), average, or single (smallest).

    The dendrogram (tree diagram) records every join; the height is the distance at which they joined. Cut it at a height to get clusters. No need to choose \(k\) in advance

    The height of a join is the distance between the clusters joined. Here three long vertical branches stand out: cutting across them gives the three types. All three linkages recover the types exactly on these data.

    Complete linkage: the distance between two clusters is that of their farthest pair

    Practical issues in clustering#

    Scale the variables to match your purpose (see “Scaling changes the answer”)

    That is slide 16.

    Linkage for hierarchical clustering: complete, average or single. Complete and average usually give balanced clusters

    Single linkage tends to chain points into long, straggly clusters.

    Clustering always finds clusters, even in random data. Check that they are stable (re-run on subsets) and meaningful

    Outliers can form their own clusters or distort the centres

    Report clusters as a starting point for investigation, not as the final truth
