    Learning without an output#

    Lectures 2–7: every example had an output \(Y\) to predict (supervised). Unsupervised: only inputs \(X_1, \dots, X_p\). No right answer to check against

    This makes unsupervised learning harder to judge: there is no test error. Its results must be checked against knowledge of the problem.

    Goals: summarise many variables with a few (PCA); find groups of similar objects (clustering)

    Group check (10 minutes): did a neural network help on your data? Your best model so far, and its CV error

    PCA is built on variance and correlation, and clustering on distance. Make sure these words are solid before the formulas.

    Variance: the spread of a variable, the average squared distance from its mean

    PCA looks for the combination of sensors with the most variance.

    Correlated: two variables that tend to rise and fall together (\(r\) near \(\pm1\)); uncorrelated: \(r = 0\)

    Correlated sensors are what PCA compresses: if flow and current always move together, one number describes both.

    Standardise: \(z = (x - \bar x)/s\), every variable gets mean 0 and variance 1

    After standardising, the total variance of 8 sensors is 8.

    Distance between two readings: \(\sqrt{\sum_j (x_j - x'_j)^2}\), as in KNN (Lecture 1)

    \(k\)-means and hierarchical clustering both measure similarity this way.

    AUC: area under the ROC curve, 1 = perfect, 0.5 = guessing (Lecture 3). False alarm: a normal case flagged as a fault (false positive, Lecture 3)

    Used to judge the PCA monitor.

    Supervised: every training example has a known output \(Y\)

    Today's methods have none: that is what “unsupervised” means (next slide).
