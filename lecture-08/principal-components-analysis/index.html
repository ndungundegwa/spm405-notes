    Principal components analysis#

    In Lectures 2–7 every example had an output \(Y\) to predict: that is supervised learning. In unsupervised learning there are only inputs \(X_1, \dots, X_p\), and no right answer to check against. That makes results harder to judge (there is no test error), so they must be checked against knowledge of the problem. There are two main goals:

    • summarise many variables with a few: principal components analysis (this page);
    • find groups of similar objects: clustering.

    Notation on this page

    Example: a pump station#

    A water pump at a utility is monitored by 8 sensors: flow, discharge pressure, suction pressure, motor current, motor power, and bearing, motor and oil temperatures. This is a typical record from a SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) system. We have 500 readings of normal operation.

    There are eight numbers per reading, but they move together. When the pump works harder, flow, pressure, current and power all rise (they are correlated); when the day is hot, all the temperatures rise. How many things are really changing?

    The idea of principal components analysis (PCA): find a few new variables, combinations of the 8 sensors, that capture most of the variation.

    Standardise first. The sensors have different units. Without standardising, the sensor with the largest numbers (for example pressure in kPa) would dominate the variance. After standardising, each sensor has variance 1, so the total variance of the 8 sensors is 8.

    Principal components#

    The first principal component is the combination

    \[ Z_1 = \phi_{11} X_1 + \phi_{21} X_2 + \dots + \phi_{p1} X_p \]

    with the largest variance, subject to \(\sum_j \phi_{j1}^2 = 1\). (Without that constraint the variance could be made as large as we like by multiplying all the \(\phi\)'s by a big number.)

    The second principal component \(Z_2\) is the combination with the largest variance among those uncorrelated with \(Z_1\); the third is uncorrelated with both; and so on. There are at most \(p\) components.

    Two words:

    • the loadings \(\phi_{jm}\) say how much each sensor contributes to component \(m\);
    • the scores \(z_{im}\) are the values of component \(m\) for each reading \(i\).

    Geometrically, the readings form a cloud of points in 8 dimensions. PC1 is the direction in which the cloud is most stretched, its longest axis; PC2 is the next longest axis, at right angles to the first.

    The mathematics

    The loading vectors are the eigenvectors of the correlation matrix of the data, and the variances of the components are the corresponding eigenvalues (ESL Section 14.5.1).

    How much does each component explain?#

    The proportion of variance explained (PVE) by a component is its variance divided by the total variance (8 here).

    A plot like this is called a scree plot (scree is the pile of rubble at the foot of a cliff: the small components after the drop). Look for the elbow, where the curve bends and flattens and later components add little. Here two components explain \(69.8 + 22.8 = 92.6\%\) of the variation.

    Worked numbers

    PC1's variance is \(0.698 \times 8 = 5.6\) standardised sensors' worth. Each noise component has about \(0.012 \times 8 = 0.1\), close to the noise variance per standardised sensor.

    Example: proportion of variance explained#

    A PCA of five standardised pump-station sensors gives the component variances (eigenvalues) 2.7, 1.2, 0.6, 0.3 and 0.2. Compute the proportion of variance explained by each component and the cumulative proportion. How many components keep at least 90%?

    The total variance of five standardised inputs is 5.0 (one per input), and indeed \(2.7 + 1.2 + 0.6 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 5.0\). Dividing each variance by 5.0:

    What do the components mean? The loadings#

    • PC1 = “load”. Flow \(+0.38\), discharge pressure \(+0.39\), current \(+0.39\) and power \(+0.39\) rise together, and suction pressure (\(-0.37\)) falls: a pump drawing more water lowers the pressure at its inlet. The temperatures also load positively (\(+0.23\) to \(+0.33\)).
    • PC2 = “temperature”. The three temperatures load strongly (\(+0.42\) to \(+0.59\)), against the hydraulic and electrical sensors (\(-0.18\) to \(-0.27\)); suction pressure has \(+0.25\).

    So there are eight sensors, but only two things really change: how hard the pump works, and how warm it is. These are exactly the two hidden factors used to simulate the data.

    The scores: each reading as two numbers#

    A plot of 2 numbers instead of 8 is easy to watch on a control-room screen. The scores can also be used as the inputs of a regression (principal components regression): with many correlated inputs, regressing on a few components is an alternative to ridge and lasso (ISLP Section 6.3.1).

    Monitoring with PCA#

    Normal readings lie close to the plane of PC1 and PC2: 92.6% of the variation is in that plane, and the rest is small noise. This gives a way to detect faults.

    Reconstruction error. Project a new reading onto the 2 components, rebuild the 8 standardised sensor values \(\hat z_j\) from the 2 scores, and measure how far off they are:

    \[ \text{error} = \sum_{j=1}^{8} \bigl(z_j - \hat z_j\bigr)^2 . \]

    Geometrically this is the squared distance from the reading to the plane (in process monitoring it is called the \(Q\) or SPE statistic). A large error means the sensors no longer agree with each other.

    Example: a partial blockage. Normally motor current and flow rise together. A blockage makes the motor work harder for less water: current up, flow down. Each sensor on its own may look normal, but together they break the usual pattern.

    We test this on 900 new normal readings and 100 readings with a blockage:

    alarm based onAUC
    the most extreme single sensor0.75
    PCA reconstruction error0.99

    Set the alarm threshold at the 99th percentile of the error on the 500 normal training readings (the value only 1% of normal readings exceed; here 1.66). Then 88% of blockages are caught, with 1.3% false alarms on new normal readings.

    No fault labels were needed: the monitor was built from normal data only, and flags anything that does not look normal. That is valuable when faults are rare and varied, as in most plants: it is a method for anomaly detection and early fault detection from sensor data.
