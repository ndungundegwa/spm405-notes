    Principal components analysis#

    Example: a pump station#

    A water pump is monitored by 8 sensors: flow; discharge pressure; suction pressure; motor current; motor power; bearing, motor and oil temperatures

    A typical supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) record at a water utility.

    Data: 500 readings of normal operation

    Eight numbers per reading, but they move together. How many things are really changing?

    When the pump works harder, flow, pressure, current and power all rise; when the day is hot, all temperatures rise.

    The idea of PCA: find a few new variables, combinations of the 8 sensors, that capture most of the variation

    Standardise first: the sensors have different units

    Otherwise the sensor with the largest numbers (for example pressure in kPa) would dominate the variance.

    Principal components#

    The first principal component is the combination \(Z_1 = \phi_{11}X_1 + \dots + \phi_{p1}X_p\) with the largest variance, where \(\sum_j\phi_{j1}^2 = 1\)

    Without the constraint, the variance could be made as large as we like by multiplying all \(\phi\) by a big number.

    The second, \(Z_2\), has the largest variance among combinations uncorrelated with \(Z_1\); and so on

    There are at most \(p\) components. Mathematically, the loading vectors are the eigenvectors of the correlation matrix of the data, and their variances are the eigenvalues (ESL 14.5.1).

    Two words: loadings \(\phi_{j1}\): how much each sensor contributes. Scores \(z_{i1}\): the value of \(Z_1\) for reading \(i\)

    Geometrically: the direction in which the cloud of points is most stretched

    In 8 dimensions the readings form a flattened cloud; PC1 is its longest axis, PC2 the next.

    Symbols

    \(Z_m\)The \(m\)-th principal component.
    \(\phi_{jm}\)Loading of sensor \(j\) on component \(m\).
    \(z_{im}\)Score of reading \(i\) on component \(m\).
    \(p\)Number of variables (8).

    How much does each component explain?#

    Plot: variance explained by each component and cumulative. PC1: 69.8%. PC2: 22.8%. PC3 to PC8: 1–2% each (noise)

    The proportion of variance explained (PVE) of a component is its variance divided by the total variance (8, after standardising).

    Scree plot: look for the elbow. Here: 2 components explain 92.6%

    The name comes from the pile of rubble (scree) at the foot of a cliff: the small components after the elbow.

    Worked numbers

    \(69.8 + 22.8 = 92.6\%\). PC1's variance is \(0.698 \times 8 = 5.6\) standardised sensors' worth; each noise component is about \(0.012 \times 8 = 0.1\), close to the noise variance per standardised sensor.

    Example: proportion of variance explained#

    A PCA of five standardised pump-station sensors gives the variances (eigenvalues) 3.2, 1.5, 0.8, 0.3 and 0.2. Compute the proportion of variance explained by each component and the cumulative proportion. How many components keep at least 90%?

    What do the components mean? The loadings#

    Bar chart: PC1 and PC2 loadings for the 8 sensors

    PC1: flow \(+0.38\), discharge pressure \(+0.39\), suction pressure \(-0.37\), current \(+0.39\), power \(+0.39\), temperatures \(+0.23\) to \(+0.33\). PC2: the three temperatures \(+0.42\) to \(+0.59\), the hydraulic and electrical sensors \(-0.18\) to \(-0.27\), suction pressure \(+0.25\).

    PC1 = “load”: flow, pressure, current and power rise together; suction pressure falls

    A pump drawing more water lowers the pressure at its inlet.

    PC2 = “temperature”: the three temperatures, against the hydraulic sensors

    Eight sensors, but only two things really change: how hard the pump works, and how warm it is

    These match the two hidden factors used to simulate the data.

    The scores: each reading as two numbers#

    Each dot is one reading of all 8 sensors. A plot of 2 numbers instead of 8: easy to watch on a control-room screen

    PCA scores can also be the inputs to a regression (principal components regression)

    With many correlated inputs, regressing on a few components is an alternative to ridge and lasso (ISLP 6.3.1).

    Monitoring with PCA#

    Normal readings lie close to the plane of PC1 and PC2

    92.6% of the variation is in that plane; the rest is small noise.

    Reconstruction error: project a new reading onto the 2 components, rebuild the 8 sensor values, and measure how far off they are: error \(= \sum_j(z_j - \hat z_j)^2\)

    \(\hat z_j\) is the value of standardised sensor \(j\) predicted from the two scores. The error is the squared distance from the reading to the plane (the \(Q\) or SPE statistic of process monitoring).

    Large error: the sensors no longer agree with each other

    A partial blockage: motor current up, but flow down. Each sensor alone looks normal; together they break the usual pattern

    Normally current and flow rise together. A blockage makes the motor work harder for less water.

    Test: 900 normal and 100 blocked readings

    Symbols

    \(z_j\)Standardised value of sensor \(j\).
    \(\hat z_j\)Its reconstruction from 2 components.

    Monitoring: the result#

    Alarm based onAUC
    the most extreme single sensor0.75
    PCA reconstruction error0.99

    Table: alarm based on the most extreme single sensor: AUC 0.75; PCA reconstruction error: AUC 0.99

    AUC as in Lecture 3.

    Alarm when the error exceeds its 99th percentile on normal data (the value only 1% of normal readings exceed): 88% of blockages caught; 1.3% false alarms

    The threshold (1.66) was set on the 500 normal training readings so that 1% of them would raise an alarm; on new normal readings the rate is 1.3%.

    No fault labels needed: the monitor was built from normal data only. It flags anything that does not look normal

    Useful when faults are rare and varied, as in most plants.

    This answers the survey problems “anomaly detection from sensor data” and “early fault detection”
